# Framing v2

## Problem statement

Every deployment waits on the same manual approval regardless of what it touches. Most deployments are routine, so the gate consumes fixed reviewer capacity on clicks and produces rubber-stamping: approvers clicking through changes they cannot meaningfully screen, and deployers waiting on changes nothing in particular is screening. As scoped with the sponsor, the problem this framing addresses is **routine-approval volume and the reviewer attention waste it produces**, manifesting as avoidable delay for routine changes.

In scope: routine-change throughput for deployers; wasted reviewer attention. Out of scope: high-risk-change latency — that is a different problem, not being solved here; the high-risk path may get *slower* as reviewers stop rubber-stamping, and the sponsor accepts that.

Manual approval is the sole control between a CI-passing change and production. Whatever changes it, two properties must remain observable: a human has looked at every change, and there is a recorded sign-off with a name attached. The sponsor adds a third guarantee the org context does not require: a named accountable party for every deployment, automated ones included — accountability-to-nobody is a regression the sponsor will not accept.

The problem is not yet one problem: it has four defensible readings (efficiency, control-quality, deployment-model, information — see Alternatives considered), and no reading is selected. The framing's deliverable is a decision document that presents the readings and what each commits the organization to; selection is deferred until the security conversation happens (see Unresolved questions).

One scoping fact constrains everything: there is no systematic record of what today's gate actually blocks. Denials, reworks, and incident attribution are not queryable, so the baseline control's detection rate is currently unmeasurable. The sponsor has committed to measuring it before any replacement claims the guarantees survived.

## Desired outcome

- **Routine changes reach production faster**: less delay attributable to the approval queue for the changes that make up the bulk of deployment volume.
- **Reviewer attention is applied where it matters**: fixed reviewer capacity is spent on changes that need human judgment; wasted attention (rubber-stamping) declines. Success is **attention quality, not hours** — the same hours with better attention counts as a win; hours saved is a proxy, not the goal.
- **The guarantees survive in observable form**: (a) human visibility of every change; (b) a recorded named sign-off; (c) the added accountability guarantee — a named accountable party for every deployment, including automated ones.
- **Safety parity is demonstrated, not asserted**: any replacement cuts over only after it is shown no worse than the measured baseline on the rare dangerous class. The shadow period this requires delays the velocity gain; the sponsor accepts that trade (safety first).
- **The high-risk cohort is explicitly not an outcome**: its latency may degrade and that is accepted as the price of restoring meaningful review.

## Relevant context

- **Ownership boundaries**: the platform/pipeline team owns the shared pipeline and the approval logic inside it; this team builds atop it. Changing the approval mechanism goes through the platform team's change process. The platform team is not yet in the room.
- **The rollout paradox**: by this project's own criteria, the first deployment of a new approval mechanism is itself a high-risk change — it alters the fate of every future deployment. It must pass human approval under the current rules before it replaces them.
- **The revert path**: reverts transit the same pipeline and the same gate. Emergency revert today is ad hoc — there is no defined override path. Under automation an override must be explicit and audited, or it becomes the side channel the constraints forbid.
- **Records reality**: approvals are logged (who, when), but denials, reworks, and incident attribution are not queryable. Any baseline measurement requires new instrumentation first.
- **The unasked stakeholder**: the security team has not been asked anything yet. Their answer — whether retrospectively inspectable automated decisions are acceptable, and for which classes — licenses or kills the automation readings as stated. That conversation is the first step; the sponsor accepts that reading selection cannot precede it. The stakeholder's inspection capacity has also not been secured.
- **Input provenance**: diff, test results, and service identity are author-supplied; pipeline stage/environment metadata and (if built) a blast-radius graph are independently generated. The audit record must capture input values *and their source*.

## Core primitives

- **routine change** — a frequency statement about what gets approved; it says nothing about danger. Not a risk category.
- **low-risk change** — criteria remain unresolved, but the direction is set by the sponsor: built from **failure mode and blast radius** (what breaks, who is affected, how fast it is caught), not from change type. Under that test the seed's own examples split: service bumps and copy tweaks qualify low; config tweaks do **not** automatically qualify (feature flags, traffic routing, and security settings have wide blast radius). The seed's lumping of config tweaks as routine was wrong and is corrected here.
- **guarantee** — the observable properties that must survive the change: a human has looked at every change; a recorded sign-off with a name attached; plus the sponsor-added guarantee of a named accountable party per deployment. The sponsor can name only the first two today; asking the approvers may extend the list.
- **baseline gate** — today's manual approval, whose detection rate is currently unmeasurable and must be instrumented before any replacement can claim parity on the guarantees.
- **shadow period** — running the classifier alongside the human gate, comparing outcomes, and cutting over only after false-negative performance on the rare dangerous class is demonstrated. It delays the velocity gain; the sponsor accepts this.
- **audit record** — must support reconstruction of the decision: policy version, every evidence input with value and source, an evaluation trace, and a named accountable party. Reproducibility, not a log line.
- **readings** — the four candidate problem-identities the decision document presents: efficiency (automate the decision for low-risk changes), control-quality (differentiated review without automating any decision), deployment-model (restructure the pipeline so routine and dangerous changes do not share a gate), information (make the informed human decision fast). No reading is selected yet.
- **deployment decision** — under a policy-only design the sponsor's default is that the policy evaluation *counts* as the decision, with a recorded accountable owner (policy owner plus deployer); otherwise idea 2 manufactures unauditable non-decisions. The final definition is the security team's terminology to own.

## Priorities

The organizational priority order applied to this problem:

1. **Safety and security governs.** Security sets the acceptable false-negative rate (against the measured baseline, not a guess); the baseline must be measured before any survival claim; the shadow period is accepted even at velocity's expense; the rollout of the new mechanism itself passes human approval under current rules; the sponsor would give security a veto over the baseline-measurement commitment.
2. **Reliability and operability.** Evidence dependability is now load-bearing: metadata that once only decorated dashboards (blast radius, service identity) becomes an input to the sole control. Provenance of inputs and the revert-path design sit here.
3. **Developer velocity.** Routine-change throughput is the driver, and is explicitly subordinated — sequencing decisions that trade velocity for safety are already made (shadow period, baseline-first).
4. **Convenience.** What the seed actually complained about, reframed: wasted attention, not hours. Attention quality is also instrumental to priorities 1–2 (rubber-stamping degrades screening), which in effect ranks it above a raw hours-saved target. No sub-ordering among the lower priorities is unknown: the org order (velocity above convenience) applies; the only nuance introduced by the sponsor's answers is the dual nature of "attention quality," stated here.

## Constraints

- **Organizational**: every deployment decision remains auditable (non-negotiable); all deployments go through the shared pipeline, no side channel; reviewer capacity is fixed and does not scale with volume; any automated approval must be inspectable after the fact by security.
- **Sponsor's reading of "no side channel"**: everything transits the one pipeline and every decision is audited. Whether *internal* gates may differ by change class is not this team's call — the platform/pipeline team owns that interpretation (open).
- **Ownership**: the approval mechanism cannot be changed without the platform team's change process, under rules not yet clarified.
- **Data**: no historical deployment-to-outcome data exists; baseline measurement requires new instrumentation (making denials, reworks, and incident attribution queryable).
- **Audit content**: policy version pinned; every evidence input with value and source (provenance required, not just values); evaluation trace; named accountable party. Pre-deploy reproducibility semantics for deployers are open (see Unresolved questions).
- **Policy governance**: a named owner (not silent absorption into the reviewer pool); policy version pinned in every audit record; policy changes get their own approval; the sponsor proposes security co-owns policy changes (unassigned today).
- **Rollout**: the new approval mechanism is treated as a high-risk change and approved by humans under the current rules before it replaces them.
- **Revert**: reverts stay on the same pipeline and gate; any emergency override must be explicit, audited, and designed not to become a side channel (no such design exists today).

## Assumptions

- **Security acceptance of automated approval with a preserved audit trail** — the seed's second assumption, still unconfirmed; the framing is instructed to carry both answers live. It must not function as a design premise before the security conversation.
- **That the two named guarantees are the complete set** manual approval provides today. The sponsor can name only two and says more may exist pending asking the approvers.
- **That low-risk changes are identifiable from information the pipeline already has.** Unproven: the definitional direction (failure mode, blast radius) is set, but the criteria have no owner, several inputs are author-supplied, and no validation data exists to test the signal.
- **That the baseline gate's blocking behavior can be made measurable with new instrumentation.** Committed in intent; the mechanics are unbuilt.
- **That attention quality serves screening quality** — i.e., that rubber-stamping actually degrades the control. This underpins the redefined success metric and the sponsor's acceptance of a slower high-risk path; it is plausible and consistent with the answers, but not yet evidenced.

## Decisions made

1. **Outcome scope**: routine-approval volume (throughput for deployers) and attention waste (for reviewers). High-risk latency is excluded as a separate problem; it may get slower, accepted.
2. **Success metric**: attention quality, not hours. "Fewer humans spent rubber-stamping" means less wasted attention; same hours with better attention is a win; hours saved is a proxy.
3. **Guarantees to preserve**: (a) a human has looked at every change; (b) a recorded named sign-off; (c) additionally, a named accountable party for every deployment including automated ones — accountability-to-nobody rejected as a regression.
4. **Low-risk direction**: defined from failure mode and blast radius, not change type; the seed's config-tweak example is corrected (config tweaks are not automatically low-risk).
5. **Baseline first**: the current gate's detection/blocking performance must be measured before any replacement claims the guarantees survived. Parity on argument alone is not acceptable; security gets a veto over that choice. New instrumentation is required since denials/reworks/incident attribution are not queryable.
6. **Deployment path**: a shadow period is mandatory — classify alongside the human gate, cut over only on demonstrated false-negative performance on the rare class. The velocity delay is accepted; safety first.
7. **Rollout self-application**: the new gate deploys under the old rules first — human-approved as a high-risk change under the current process before it replaces that process.
8. **Audit bar**: reconstruction-grade records — policy version, every evidence input with value and source, evaluation trace, named accountable party.
9. **False-negative threshold**: security owns it; set against the measured baseline, not a guess; not set by the sponsor alone.
10. **Policy governance**: named owner required; policy version pinned per audit record; policy changes separately approved; security co-ownership of policy changes proposed (unassigned).
11. **Framing deliverable**: a decision document presenting the readings and what each commits the organization to; no reading selected. Reading selection (D1) is deferred until the security conversation (D2) is answered.
12. **Deployment-model redesign**: in scope as a reading to be evaluated, not a committed direction; if selected, it becomes its own workstream with the platform team. For this stage: on the table, not designed.
13. **Input provenance is a design constraint**: audit records capture input values and their sources; mixing independently generated signals (pipeline stage/environment metadata, blast-radius graph if built) is required.
14. **Default for policy-only decisions**: the policy evaluation counts as the deployment decision, with a recorded accountable owner (policy owner plus deployer) — the sponsor's position against idea 2 manufacturing unauditable non-decisions; subject to security's ownership of the term.
15. **Classification consumer is explicitly unmade**: the seed's open question is carried with both consumers — machine classifier and human deciding quickly — because the evidence bar differs materially between them. The earlier implicit machine-consumer assumption is withdrawn.
16. **Uniformity suspicion, not commitment**: the sponsor suspects uniform application of one approval rule is the root cause (tiering, routing, and batching could reclaim attention without touching the approval concept), but is not committing the framing to the deployment-model reading.

## Alternatives considered

- **Idea 1 — classify by risk, auto-approve low-risk** (efficiency reading). Live. Gated on the security answer and an owned low-risk definition; delivers throughput directly; carries sole-control replacement risk and the gameability of a learnable boundary.
- **Idea 2 — drop approval for qualifying deployments, enforce guarantees entirely in automated policy** (efficiency reading, policy variant). Live. Hardest audit problem (what is the decision when no human decides — sponsor's default: the policy evaluation itself). Its failure mode is silent by construction: policy gaps are not recorded errors. Requires complete enumeration of the guarantees, which is strictly harder than preserving them.
- **Idea 3 — keep approval as an explicit state; AI/policy recommendation** (control-quality reading). Live, but split: **advisory UI** (decision maker unchanged — the seed's complaint persists; absorbed by the information reading below) vs. **default-approve-with-a-window** (a de facto automation and must then meet the automation bar of ideas 1/2).
- **Reading 3 — deployment-model change.** Two sub-variants: a separate fast lane (collides head-on with "no side channel") vs. restructuring inside the pipeline (the auditable decision becomes classification or staged rollout; rewrites "approval" in org terminology). Feasibility depends on the platform team's interpretation of the constraints; they are not in the room.
- **Reading 4 — information problem: faster informed human decisions** via tiering, routing, batching, better pre-review evidence — without touching the approval concept. Compatible with every org constraint as written; the sponsor endorsed that these can reclaim attention without renegotiation. Ceiling: may not reduce hours much — acceptable under the redefined success metric.
- **Set aside**: hours-saved as the success metric; high-risk latency as part of this problem; committing to any reading before the security conversation; the seed's lumping of config tweaks as low-risk; asserting parity on argument alone without baseline measurement.

## Design proposal — classify-and-auto-approve

### Approach

This proposal designs the efficiency reading from the framing — Idea 1, "classify by risk, auto-approve low-risk" — as a *decision-record-first* system: a deterministic, versioned policy evaluator that classifies every deployment into exactly one of two outcomes, `LOW-RISK` (auto-approved) or `REVIEW` (human gate, unchanged), and that writes a reconstruction-grade decision record for every deployment regardless of outcome. The core design commitments, stated so later agents can challenge them precisely:

1. **The classifier is deterministic policy evaluation, not a learned model.** The framing records that "no historical deployment-to-outcome data exists," so any learned classifier has no labels to train on — and a learned classifier cannot produce the "evaluation trace" the audit bar demands (framing core primitive: "Reproducibility, not a log line"). The acceptance criterion is false-negative performance on "the rare dangerous class" — a class with tiny counts that must be *right by construction* on the enumerable axes (blast radius, failure mode, detection), not statistically calibrated on data that does not exist. A learned component is not ruled out forever; it is gated behind labeled outcomes that only the measurement loop this design builds can produce.

2. **Binary classification with an asymmetric authority model: the machine may only affirm low risk, never assert danger.** The classifier outputs `LOW-RISK` (an affirmative, positive safety claim) or `REVIEW` (a declination to assert). It never denies; denial is a human act. This is the consequence of the framing's correction that low-risk is built "from failure mode and blast radius… not from change type": those axes can support an affirmative boundedness claim ("blast radius is mechanically provable to be within this set"), but the machine cannot defend the inverse ("this is dangerous") and is not asked to.

3. **`LOW-RISK` is a conjunction, not a score.** Every safety-relevant predicate must hold affirmatively, with independently generated evidence; any missing, stale, or author-only evidence fails the change closed to `REVIEW`. A score threshold buys off a wide blast radius with clean tests — exactly the wrong trade for this criterion — and gives policy erosion a dial to turn. The conjunction makes each rule individually falsifiable: a divergence event blames a specific rule, which is what makes the shadow period a policy-debugging loop rather than a staring contest (see Failure modes F10).

4. **Every deployment gets a decision record, and the auto-approval *is* the decision.** This adopts the sponsor's default from framing decision 14 verbatim: "the policy evaluation counts as the deployment decision, with a recorded accountable owner (policy owner plus deployer)." There are no non-decisions; `REVIEW` decisions carry the same record shape and later receive appended human dispositions. The auto-approved deployment's "recorded named sign-off" (guarantee b) *is* the decision record with its named accountable parties.

5. **The system fails closed to today's state in every direction.** Engine outage, missing evidence, unresolvable identity, policy suspension — all degrade to everything-requires-human-approval, which is exactly the status quo. The worst operational failure of this design is no throughput gain. It cannot produce an open gate.

6. **One guarantee is modified, not preserved, and the design says so.** Guarantee (a) — "a human has looked at every change" — does not survive for the auto-approved class in per-change form. This reading replaces per-change pre-deploy human review of that class with: machine evaluation with a full trace, ledger visibility of every auto-approval, a security-tuned standing sample of human re-review, and automatic security escalation of any auto-approved deployment implicated in an incident. This is the concession the reading makes; the compensation is explicit and designed, but whether it satisfies the security team's definition of the guarantee surviving "in observable form" is exactly framing question 1, and **live mode is conditional on that answer. Shadow mode is not** — a permanent-shadow state is a stable, safe outcome of this design (it costs pipeline compute and has already instrumented the baseline). The design contains its own rejection path.

7. **What this design assumes, and does not resolve:** it assumes the security team licenses retrospective-inspection-based automated decisions for the low-risk class under the audit bar below (framing Q1); it assumes the platform team's interpretation of "no side channel" permits a class-differentiated internal gate *inside* the shared pipeline (framing Q4) — if it does not, this reading is dead, which is a fork to report, not a blocker to design around; it adopts the sponsor's default on the auditable unit (framing Q5, D14) pending security's terminology; it leaves the low-risk criteria *values* unowned (framing Q2) and the FNR threshold unset (framing Q6) — this design delivers the mechanism and the criterion *shape*, not the numbers, and live mode cannot begin until both have owners.

### System primitives

**Evidence object.** The smallest unit of truth the policy may read: `{id, kind, value, source-class, origin, capture-time, integrity, derived-from[]}` where `source-class ∈ {author-supplied, pipeline-derived, pipeline-observed}`. Author-supplied values are claims (diff contents, declared intent, test config); pipeline-derived values are mechanically computed from attested artifacts (change-class, affected services); pipeline-observed values are statements of fact made by pipeline systems with identity (environment, test execution results, recent history). Every object binds by hash to the **deployment artifact** it describes. This taxonomy operationalizes the framing's input-provenance decision (D13): "the audit record must capture input values *and their source.*"

**Decision context (snapshot).** The complete, frozen set of evidence objects available to one evaluation — per-change evidence, environment facts, and a bounded window of pipeline history for the actor and affected services. Evaluation reads *only* the snapshot: no lazy fetches, no wall clock, no external calls, no random. The snapshot is the reproducibility unit.

**Policy version.** An immutable, content-addressed document: the conjunctive rule set, the evidence requirements per rule (including which inputs *must* be pipeline-derived), the accountability mapping, the mode (`shadow` | `live`), the effective window, and the named owner list. Identified as `POLICY-<content-hash>`. Policy changes are separately human-approved (framing constraint: "policy changes get their own approval") and a substantive change resets the shadow clock — shadow evidence is earned per policy version, never inherited.

An illustrative skeleton of the conjunctive clauses, to make the mechanism concrete (criteria *values* are the unowned work, framing Q2; the *shape* is this design's proposal):

1. `artifact-integrity` — evaluated artifact ≡ deployed artifact (attested by pipeline). [pipeline-observed]
2. `change-class-bounded` — change composition, mechanically derived from the diff, falls in an enumerated bounded set (e.g., content, docs, pinned-image bumps, pure refactors). Declared type is never a policy input. [pipeline-derived from author-supplied diff]
3. `blast-radius-bounded` — affected services, derived from the pipeline-maintained blast-radius graph, ⊆ the policy's low-blast-radius set. [pipeline-derived]
4. `failure-detectable` — each affected service has monitoring coverage for the change class's failure-mode family with a detection SLO better than the policy threshold. [pipeline-observed]
5. `tests-affirmative` — relevant tests pass; coverage not degraded on touched modules. [pipeline-observed execution, author-configured selection — the distinction is recorded]
6. `no-special-infra` — diff composition touches none of: feature flags, traffic routing, security settings, credentials, data migrations, infrastructure code, or the **never-auto domain**. [pipeline-derived]
7. `history-clean` — no open incident on affected services; actor and service auto-approval cadences below policy thresholds. [pipeline-observed]
8. `identity-attested` — deployer identity attested by the pipeline's auth system, resolving to a named human (directly or via tool-account owner registry). [pipeline-observed]
9. `mode-live` — this change class is in `live` mode under graduated cutover. [policy]

All nine affirmatively hold → `LOW-RISK`. Any failure → `REVIEW`. Each rule names its evidence requirement; a rule satisfied only by author-supplied evidence fails by construction. Note rule 8: a service-account deploy with no resolvable named owner cannot be `LOW-RISK`, because "accountability-to-nobody is a regression the sponsor will not accept" — the named accountable party is a precondition of auto-approval, not a decoration on it.

**Evaluation trace.** The ordered, total walk: every rule, the evidence object references it consumed (with source-class), its outcome, and the derivation of the final classification. This is what "reconstruction-grade" cashes out to. The trace is also the deployer-facing artifact (which rules failed, on what evidence) and the reviewer's pre-digested brief.

**Decision record.** `{decision-id, classification, mode, policy-version, evaluator-digest, snapshot-ref, trace-ref, accountable-parties, timestamp, artifact-hash}`. Append-only; status evolves by appended records: `pending-human → human-approved | denied | reworked`, `auto-approved → reverted | implicated-in-incident | overridden`. Dispositions (denial, rework, approval) are first-class records — this is the instrumentation that makes the baseline queryable (framing: "Denials, reworks, and incident attribution are not queryable" today).

**Accountable party.** `{role ∈ {deployer, policy-owner}, identity, resolution-method ∈ {auth-attested, registry-mapped, policy-declared}, resolution-time}`. Per framing D14: deployer plus policy owner, both named humans. Self-declaration never resolves identity; only attestation or registry mapping does.

**Never-auto domain.** Change classes structurally excluded from auto-approval by the meta-policy, regardless of risk logic: the classifier's own code, the policy corpus, the evaluator, the ledger, pipeline control code, and credential/infra material. This is the standing form of the rollout self-application decision (D7): the classifier can never evaluate its own release. It is not a risk judgment; it is a hard exclusion checked by change composition.

**Measurement events.** `divergence` (shadow disagreement: classifier `LOW-RISK`, human denied/reworked — the shadow false-negative signal); `dangerous-class event` (denial/rework, or deployment-to-incident attribution via the new queryable pipeline); `override` (explicit human force-approval); `boundary-nearness` (derived margin metric for drift and probing watch, never a decision input).

**FNR monitor.** The standing computation of observed false-negative performance against the security-set threshold, fed by divergences (shadow) and dangerous-class events plus sampled re-review (live).

### Architecture

Seven components, in two trust domains.

**Trust domain A — this team builds and owns:** *(1) Evidence ingestion* normalizes pipeline events into evidence objects and performs the trust conversion (author claims are tagged, never laundered); *(2) the decision engine* — a pure function `(policy-version, evaluator-digest, snapshot) → trace + classification`, deterministic to the byte; *(3) the decision store (ledger)* — append-only, write-access restricted to the engine and the disposition-capture hook, read policy by role, replay-enabled; *(4) the measurement loop* — shadow metrics, FNR reports, drift and cadence alarms; *(5) sampled re-review* — post-cutover standing human review of a security-set fraction of auto-approvals, disagreement-tracked; *(6) policy governance* — policy changes as PRs with named human approval (owner + security), a replay-regression preview over the historical corpus (any classification delta not caused by an intentional policy change is surfaced to the owners as a review item), release as a new immutable version.

**Trust domain B — platform team owns:** *(7) the mode controller* — the pipeline stage that consults the engine and enforces: in `shadow`, classification is recorded and the human gate proceeds exactly as today; in `live`, `LOW-RISK` proceeds on the recorded decision, `REVIEW` proceeds to the unchanged human gate. The controller is the only actuator; there is no other path. Its existence depends on the platform team's interpretation of "no side channel" (framing Q4, stated here as a design assumption, not resolved).

**Integrity spine.** Every evidence object binds to the artifact hash; the controller enforces and the record attests `evaluated-artifact ≡ deployed-artifact`. This kills the evaluate-clean-deploy-dirty attack and pins the decision to *this* artifact, so the audit claim "the thing that was approved is the thing that ran" is mechanically true, not asserted. This invariant is platform-enforced; its attestation is this team's.

**Determinism requirements.** The policy language is a small, total DSL; totality and determinism are enforced by construction (no wall clock, no RNG, no iteration over unordered structures, no evaluation-time I/O — snapshot only). If code-as-policy is preferred, the corpus passes a determinism lint and the evaluator is content-addressed and pinned by digest; the record stores the digest. This is what makes framing's "inspectable after the fact by security" real: any security engineer can replay any decision from `(policy-version, evaluator-digest, snapshot)` and reproduce the trace byte-for-byte. Replay-regression over the corpus gates every evaluator release.

**Failure behavior.** The engine's availability governs only auto-approval, not the gate: engine down, evidence gap, identity unresolvable → `REVIEW` → today's manual flow. The gate's availability is unchanged from today; the new system is strictly an overlay that removes review load in `live` mode and adds none in `shadow`.

**Phasing.** P0 — baseline instrumentation (denials, reworks, incident attribution queryable; this is the framing's precondition for any survival claim). P1 — recording shadow: the classifier evaluates and records every deployment from pipeline read access; zero enforcement surface, no platform gate change needed. P2 — measured shadow: disposition capture hooks (a platform change) enable divergence measurement; the zero-divergence streak per policy version accumulates; divergences feed policy revision, each substantive revision resetting the clock. P3 — graduated cutover: `live` mode enabled per change class, smallest class first, each expansion a human-approved policy change under current rules. P1/P2 maximize pre-platform-team construction; P3 is contingent on framing Q4 and Q11.

### User workflows

**Deployer.** Push → pipeline runs → evaluation happens with the rest of the gates. On `REVIEW`: unchanged human gate, but the trace shows exactly which rules failed and on what evidence — actionable pre-review feedback. On `LOW-RISK` (live mode): the deployment proceeds on the decision record; identity was attested upstream, not self-declared. Positions taken on framing Q8 (pre-deploy feedback): the classification and trace are always visible to the deployer; the policy text is readable. Rationale: a learnable boundary is learnable by probing regardless of secrecy, so gameability is fought at the evidence level (mechanical derivation), not by opacity — which would destroy deployer legibility and guarantee inspectability violations downstream.

**Reviewer.** In shadow: identical to today, plus an instrumentation obligation they will not feel. Post-cutover: the queue contains only `REVIEW`-class changes plus the sampled re-review items; every review screen shows the trace. The framing has already accepted the high-risk cohort may get *slower*; this design does not promise otherwise. It does promise an upper bound in the other direction: the classifier only ever removes items from the queue. The only added review load is the deliberate measurement tax of sampled re-review, set by security.

**Policy owner.** Writes policy changes as PRs; co-approval with security; replay-regression preview; release as immutable version. The role is real work, not a title: policy owner is the accountable human on every auto-approved deployment under their version, and the FNR of their policy is their aggregate record. The role is currently unassigned (framing Q9); live mode cannot start without it.

**Security.** Three standing surfaces: the ledger query/replay tooling (every decision, every auto-approval in a window, one-command replay); the measurement reports (FNR against threshold, divergences, overrides, drift alarms); and override notifications (100% back-review, below). Plus the threshold itself, as a parameter only security can set — including, explicitly, a threshold that never triggers, which this design is fully compatible with (permanent shadow).

**Incident responder.** Incident review links implicated deployments by decision-id. An auto-approved implication auto-escalates to security with mandatory retro review and flags the admitting policy version in the FNR panel.

### Failure modes

**F1. Classifier false negative (a dangerous change classified `LOW-RISK`).** The acceptance criterion itself; residual risk is bounded by construction (conjunction, independent evidence, fail-closed on gaps) and measured by the loop, never eliminated — a threshold of zero is a legitimate security setting, not a design failure. The honest epistemic limit is stated in F10.

**F2. Evidence manipulation (author falsifies a claim).** Addressed by provenance: author-supplied inputs can never satisfy a safety predicate; all risk-relevant facts are mechanically derived or pipeline-observed; claim/observation mismatch is itself a policy outcome (`blast-radius-bounded` fails when the graph and the diff disagree) → `REVIEW`.

**F3. Policy-boundary gameability (shaping or splitting changes to pass).** Addressed by: mechanical derivation of change-class and blast radius (declarations are invisible to the policy); the actor/service cadence rule, which routes anomalous auto-approval bursts to `REVIEW`; near-miss and drift monitoring of boundary-nearness; and the never-auto domain, which cannot be shaped around. The residual risk — a determined attacker decomposing a dangerous change into individually-low-risk-shaped units — is partly bounded by the sequence context (rule 7 reads history), and is a standing item for the security conversation, not a silently accepted hole.

**F4. Policy rot (decay of service lists, monitoring coverage, change-class meanings).** Mitigated by policy version expiry (a version has a max lifetime; lapse fails closed to `REVIEW`), continuous recomputation of the blast-radius graph, drift alarms on classification distributions, and the standing sampled re-review as an independent decay detector.

**F5. Accountability evaporation.** Identity unresolvable → `REVIEW` (rule 8). Policy owner departs without successor → version suspended → auto-approval stops. Accountability lapse fails closed.

**F6. The override/revert side channel.** Framing: "under automation an override must be explicit and audited, or it becomes the side channel the constraints forbid." Design: `OVERRIDE` is a decision-record type on the *same path* — a named human, a structured justification, mandatory 100% post-hoc security review (not statistical sampling), rate alarms with escalation, and each override is entered into the FNR panel as a dangerous-class event. An override is literally a classifier false negative caught by a human; the design converts it from a hole into signal. There is no separate override path.

**F7. Evaluator bugs, especially loosening bugs.** A rule bug that lets changes through is silent by construction. Mitigated by: replay-regression over the full historical corpus on every evaluator release (any classification delta not caused by the intended policy change surfaces to the owners); evaluator changes run shadow-first for a quota before their policy versions go live; and the trace is the debugging instrument when the loop flags a pattern.

**F8. TOCTOU (evaluate-clean, deploy-dirty).** Killed by the artifact-hash integrity spine.

**F9. Ledger compromise.** Append-only store, write-restricted to the engine and the disposition hook, no mutation/redaction (corrections are appended records), retention per policy. An attacker who can write arbitrary decision records is by definition an attacker who owns the pipeline — out of scope for a gate design, but the write-authorization model is the honest first line.

**F10. The shadow period undercounts.** The shadow oracle is the human gate, and the human gate rubber-stamps — that is the premise of this project. Shadow "parity" is agreement with a weak oracle, not ground truth; dangerous changes that sail through both classifier and rubber-stamping human are invisible at the time and surface only later via incident attribution and sampled re-review. **Therefore the cutover criterion is deliberately not a statistical equivalence test** (see Alternatives): it is a zero-divergence streak per policy version, over a minimum volume *per change class* and minimum duration, against the measured baseline that instrumentation finally provides, with security setting the numbers. The design states plainly what it can and cannot demonstrate: it demonstrates the classifier never disagrees with a human in the dangerous direction over the shadow corpus; it cannot, from shadow alone, demonstrate the absolute FNR.

**F11. Guarantee (a) erosion.** The modified guarantee is designed compensation (Approach §6), not preservation. This mode of the design is the one most exposed to the security conversation, and the design's honesty here is the point.

### Operational complexity

**Degradation behavior is the headline simplicity.** Every failure mode resolves to fewer auto-approvals, never to a bypassed gate. Runbooks are monotonous by design: "auto-approval unavailable → manual queue, same as before."

**The ledger and replay tooling are the real standing cost.** Diffs and snapshots grow unboundedly; retention is policy-set, derived evidence is compact and replay-grade, raw diffs are retained under access control (see Security S4). Replay is deterministic parallel compute over an ever-growing corpus — cheap, but it is a standing batch burden that needs an owner.

**The measurement tax on fixed reviewer capacity.** Sampled re-review consumes hours from the very pool the design exists to unburden. This is correct under the framing's success metric — "same hours with better attention counts as a win" — but it is a budget line the sponsor must carve explicitly, and the rate is security's to set. If the rate is set high enough, the design can *spend* its entire throughput win on measurement and still win on attention quality; that is a feature, not a pathology.

**Role staffing.** Policy owner, measurement-loop operator, re-review cadence — three roles, currently zero assigned owners (framing Q9). The PoC can run P1/P2 with one owner; live mode cannot run with none.

**The loop is itself a data pipeline and can lie.** Incident attribution is human-confirmed in the normal incident process made queryable, and sampled re-review is an independent signal that does not share its error modes; the loop's own reports carry confidence bounds, and the loop itself needs periodic audit — an unmeasured measurement system would quietly invalidate the acceptance criterion it feeds.

### Security implications

**S1. The classifier is an adversarial surface.** Deployers gain real value from a `LOW-RISK` classification, so the boundary will be probed. The design's answer is structural: the policy cannot be satisfied by declarations (mechanical derivation only), and probing is visible — near-miss traces and boundary-nearness trends are queryable by security and are themselves a signal worth watching for coordinated gaming.

**S2. Accountability is only as strong as identity.** The deployer in the decision record is the pipeline-attested actor, and tool accounts must resolve through an owner registry to a named human or the change cannot be `LOW-RISK` (rule 8). If the platform team's identity model allows forgeable actor labels, this entire design's accountability guarantee inherits that weakness — this is a platform-team dependency to state in the negotiation, not a hole this team can paper over.

**S3. Inspection after the fact is the license for everything.** The design cashes out framing Q1's condition as: an append-only, replayable ledger; a one-command replay for any historical decision; write authorization the operating team itself cannot use to alter a record; and retention policy-set. If security defines "inspectable" as more than this (e.g., sampled exceptions only, or per-change human confirmation, or dual-sign-off), the design degrades — to shadow-forever or to dead — which is the framing's stated fork, not a design failure.

**S4. Secrets in audit records.** The retained raw diff may contain secrets. Resolution: the policy reads only derived structural evidence, never raw diff contents; raw artifacts are retained under access control for inspection, with presentation-layer scrubbing for the normal audience. Replay needs only the snapshot and derived evidence, not raw content — record completeness and secret hygiene do not conflict.

**S5. The meta-gate must never be the classifier.** Policy, evaluator, ledger, and pipeline-control changes are never auto-approvable — the never-auto domain, hard-coded in the meta-policy. An attacker who can push a policy change through any automated path has remotely approved themselves; the policy governance pipeline's human approvals are the control that must not itself be automated. This is the standing generalization of the rollout self-application decision (D7).

**S6. The FNR threshold is security's configuration, and the design runs at any setting.** Including zero (no cutover — stable shadow state) and including settings security derives from the measured baseline. The measurement loop reports are produced by a system distinct from the policy governance pipeline, so the team whose system is being measured cannot author its own passing grades.

**S7. The composition of the post-cutover control.** Safety is the *system's* miss rate, decomposed: `P(classifier approves dangerous) + P(classifier defers AND human misses dangerous)`. The first term is measured in shadow (with F10's caveat) and kept below threshold; the second is plausibly *better* than today's because the review queue loses the rubber-stamp volume — the framing's attention-quality thesis, here doing real safety work. The design's safety case is honest about its weakest point: both terms are known only within the precision of the oracle and the instrumentation, and the first term's measurement is bounded by the second term's quality during shadow.

### Implementation constraints

- **Platform team dependency for any enforcement.** P1 (recording shadow) needs only pipeline read access — the lightest possible ask. P2 needs disposition capture in the human gate UI. P3 needs the mode controller inside the pipeline stage. All three are the platform team's change process, and the framing records they are "not yet in the room" (framing Q4, Q11). The phasing is ordered so that maximum construction precedes the heavier negotiations.
- **Security conversation gates live mode, not construction.** P0–P2 are buildable and valuable with zero security concessions: P0 delivers the baseline measurement the framing has already committed to (D5), and P2 delivers the divergence-learned policy even if cutover never happens.
- **Determinism is a build-time property.** A total deterministic DSL, or linted code-as-policy with content-addressed evaluator and pinned digest. This must be enforced from the first commit; retrofit determinism is not a thing.
- **Criteria values remain unowned.** The policy skeleton above is mechanism; the actual service sets, monitoring SLOs, cadence thresholds, and class lists are the unresolved Q2 work. Live mode must not start with placeholder values, because placeholders are how a low-risk boundary silently becomes a rubber stamp with a machine's signature.
- **Reviewer-hour budget for the measurement tax** must be carved from the fixed pool explicitly, before cutover, or sampled re-review will silently starve the human gate it is supposed to verify.
- **Evaluator releases are pipeline releases** with replay-regression over the corpus as a gating test — this is a non-negotiable build requirement, not an optimization.

### Alternatives considered

**A1. A learned ML classifier.** Rejected: no outcome labels exist ("no historical deployment-to-outcome data exists"); a learned model cannot emit the reconstruction-grade evaluation trace the audit bar requires; and the rare dangerous class is exactly the regime where statistical calibration is least trustworthy. Not excluded forever — the measurement loop this design builds produces the labels a learned component would need — but it cannot be the first classifier.

**A2. A risk score with a threshold.** Rejected: scores create a tunable boundary whose adjustment feels operational rather than governance-grade, and they permit compensation across axes (clean tests buying off wide blast radius) — precisely wrong when the criterion is conjunctive safety. The conjunction gives every failure a named rule, which is what makes shadow divergences actionable.

**A3. Random-sampled throttle as the cutover mechanism.** Rejected: a sampling gate makes enforcement a lottery, complicates the decision semantics ("this identical low-risk change was auto-approved; that one was not"), and adds machinery for no measurement value. Cutover is graduated *by change class* — each expansion a human-approved policy change, coherent with "policy changes get their own approval" (D10). Random sampling survives only in its correct role: the standing post-cutover re-review *measurement* mechanism, which never affects enforcement.

**A4. Statistical equivalence as the shadow cutover test.** Rejected in F10: equivalence against a rubber-stamping oracle manufactures parity with a weak control. The cutover criterion is a zero-divergence streak plus per-class volume and duration, conservatively set — deliberately demonstrating too much to pass rather than barely enough.

**A5. Incident attribution as the only post-cutover signal.** Rejected: attribution is slow, late, and confounded; the design needs the faster disagreement signal of sampled re-review to detect live FNR drift before incidents accumulate.

**A6. Secret policy to defeat gameability.** Rejected: the boundary is learnable by probing regardless; secrecy destroys deployer legibility and conflicts with the inspectability license. The gameability defense is mechanical derivation of evidence, not opacity of rules.

**A7. No-decision semantics for auto-approved changes** (Idea 2's flavor: the deployment just proceeds). Rejected within this direction: it manufactures "unauditable non-decisions," which the sponsor's framing default explicitly rejects (D14). Every deployment gets a decision record; `LOW-RISK` is a *decision*, with named parties, not an absence of one.

**A8. Three-way classification with a machine-asserted dangerous class.** Rejected: the machine cannot defend a dangerousness assertion its policy cannot enumerate; denial stays a human act, which keeps the authority model clean (the machine affirms; humans deny) and keeps accountability for the high-stakes direction where it belongs.

**A9. A separate override path for emergencies.** Rejected: a second path is the side channel the framing forbids ("or it becomes the side channel the constraints forbid"). Overrides are a decision-record type on the one path, with forced 100% review, which converts them into measurement signal instead of a hole.

## Design proposal — policy-replaces-approval

### Approach

The direction: **for qualifying deployments, eliminate the approval concept — not re-cast it.** A deployment that satisfies the machine-checkable policy proceeds; there is no approval stage, no approver, no approval record of any kind. The framing's own resolution applies verbatim: "the policy evaluation counts as the deployment decision, with a recorded accountable owner" (D14). The record of that evaluation — the **qualification certificate** — *is* the decision record, with named accountable parties. The second sentence of the direction does the real work: **"guarantees become policy rules."** This design takes that sentence literally, and designs the machinery that makes literalness auditable.

The framing warns what this direction costs: "requires complete enumeration of the guarantees, which is strictly harder than preserving them," and "its failure mode is silent by construction: policy gaps are not recorded errors." Both are engaged head-on, not minimized. The design's answer to enumeration is a ratification process and an admission; its answer to silence is scoped claims plus instruments that measure the silence itself.

**Core commitments** (stated precisely so later agents can challenge them):

1. **The guarantee-version is the foundation, and its completeness is governed, never proven.** Every qualified deployment's claim references a named guarantee-version. Completeness cannot be established by any record or replay — a guarantee nobody has articulated leaves no trace for inspection to find. The design therefore treats "the guarantee list is complete" as a standing hypothesis, sustained by elicitation, representational audit, divergence review, and incident reopening, and *stated as a hypothesis in every certificate's scoped claim* (S2). This is the direction's irreducible residual; it is not engineered away.

2. **Compilation is explicit and lossy, and the loss is recorded.** Each guarantee maps to policy rule(s) through a signed **traceability matrix**. Where compilation modifies a guarantee's meaning, the matrix says so (`modified`), with the mechanism of the modification named. A guarantee that compiles losslessly is marked `preserved` and carries that proof burden — a burden only one of the three named guarantees survives (g3, below). A guarantee that cannot be compiled at all is a **guard error**: the qualifying path cannot operate while one exists.

3. **Qualification is a decision, not a non-decision.** D14 settles the audit problem the framing flags ("what is the decision when no human decides"): the evaluation is the decision. The certificate is appended to the same ledger as human dispositions, with the same insistence on named humans. Nobody said "go" — the rules admitted it — and exactly two names stand attached to that admission.

4. **Claim scoping is schema-enforced, so the record cannot lie by implication.** The certificate's claim string is *constructed* from its guarantee-version and matrix reference: "all guarantees in guarantee-version G were enforced for artifact A under policy-version P." It is structurally incapable of asserting "the organization's guarantees were enforced" — because the record does not contain a field that could hold that assertion. A scoped truth is the only truth the record can tell.

5. **The human presence is relocated, not deleted.** Guarantee (a) — "a human has looked at every change" — does not survive in per-change form for the qualified class; the framing's question is whether it survives "in observable form." This design's observable form is fourfold: humans author and sign the rules every change is evaluated against (per-rule); a standing security-set fraction of qualified deployments gets full human re-review (sampled per-change); any human can object to a qualified deployment through an on-path, audited channel (reactive per-change); and any qualified deployment implicated in an incident receives mandatory human review (reactive per-change). These are not compensations bolted onto the direction — they are *the compiled form of guarantee (a)*. Dropping any of them is an unrecorded guarantee violation, not a budget decision.

6. **Fail-closed everywhere.** Engine outage, evidence gap, unresolvable identity, policy lapse, unstaffed role → the deployment fails to qualify and transits the unchanged human approval path. The worst operational failure of this design is the status quo plus a slower queue. It cannot produce an open gate.

7. **The encodability tracker is the direction's falsification instrument.** Every shadow divergence — a deployment the policy qualified, a human denied or reworked — is human-classified: *encodable* (the divergence names a rule or predicate gap, which becomes a matrix-governed policy change) or *non-encodable* (the human's judgment does not reduce to rules). A standing **encodability ratio** measures persistent non-encodable residue, and security may set a threshold above which live mode is revoked. The earlier proposal built a shadow loop for tuning a classifier; this design's shadow loop additionally *tests the direction's own premise* — that human judgment can be compiled. If it cannot, the direction should die, and this is the instrument that says so.

**The semantic home of the screening predicates.** Composition bounding, blast-radius bounding, detection coverage, cleanliness, history — these predicate families are near-identical in content to the classifier rules of the earlier proposal. Under this direction they are *not a risk claim*. They are the **compiled residue of guarantee (a)**: the encodable part of "what a human would look at," bound into the matrix under g1's `modified` compilation note. Passing them does not mean "this change is safe." It means the encodable portion of the human-looking guarantee was enforced on this change. The distinction governs everything downstream: governance weight, audit claim semantics, and failure narratives.

**Assumptions and non-resolutions.** This direction's deepest gate is framing Q1 in its hardest form: security must accept *rule-governed admission* — no human decision anywhere in the qualified path — with scoped claims and named accountability. Q4 is a sharper ask than the earlier proposal made: the platform team must permit the pipeline's approval stage to be *bypassed* for qualified deployments, not merely satisfied upstream. Q5 adopts D14 pending security's terminology. Q2, Q6, Q9, Q10 remain unmet: criteria values, the violation-rate threshold, policy ownership, and — fatally for live mode — the guarantee list itself. The sponsor's three guarantees (g1–g3) are the initial *candidate* list, not a closed enumeration; the framing records the approvers have not been asked. This design treats asking them as Phase 0, and treats their answer as a release artifact.

### System primitives

**Guarantee object.** `{id, statement, provenance, version, status}` — where `provenance` records the elicitation source (which approvers, what session), date, and signers; `version` is monotonic; `status ∈ {proposed, ratified, superseded, revoked}`. Ratification requires named humans (guarantee body, below) and is the highest-authority act in the system: a guarantee-version bump redefines what "all guarantees enforced" means for every subsequent certificate.

**Traceability matrix.** Per policy version: one row per guarantee in the effective guarantee-version: `{guarantee-id, compiled-rules[], evidence-requirements, compilation-note}` where `compilation-note ∈ {preserved, modified(mechanism), uncompiled}`. `uncompiled` is a guard error — the policy version cannot govern a qualified path. The matrix is signed by policy owner plus security; policy versions cannot be released without it. The concrete compilation this design proposes, as the initial candidate:

| Guarantee | Compiled form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| g1 — a human has looked at every change | r1: governing policy version within its effective window, human-authored and signed [per-rule]; r2: standing sampled human re-review of qualified deployments at a security-set rate [sampled per-change]; r3: objection channel open, monitored, SLO-bound [reactive per-change]; r4: qualified deployments implicated in incidents receive mandatory human review [reactive per-change]; **plus** the screening predicates — composition-bounded, blast-radius-bounded, failure-detectable, no-special-infra, history-clean, artifact-integrity — as the encodable residue | **modified.** Per-change pre-deploy human look is dropped for the qualified class. The per-rule, sampled, and reactive forms replace it. |
| g2 — recorded sign-off with a name | r5: certificate exists, with accountable parties (deployer + policy owner), both resolving to named humans by attestation or registry | **modified.** The sign-off is no longer a human act; it is a recorded accountability fact. If security's definition of sign-off requires a human act, the direction fails for the qualified class (Q1). |
| g3 — named accountable party per deployment | r6: both parties resolve to named humans; self-declaration never resolves identity; unresolvable identity fails qualification | **preserved.** Compiles losslessly. |

The asymmetry is the design's honest picture: the only guarantee that survives intact is the one the sponsor added *because it was expressible in compiled form from birth* ("accountability-to-nobody is a regression the sponsor will not accept," and the pipeline can already attest identity). The inherited human-process guarantees resist compilation; the design says so in the matrix rather than smuggling the loss.

**Qualification certificate (the decision record).** `{decision-id, artifact-hash, policy-version, guarantee-version, matrix-ref, claim, per-guarantee-outcome[], trace-ref, snapshot-ref, accountable-parties[], timestamp, objection-state}`. The `claim` field is *constructed* at evaluation time from the named versions — the schema has no free-text claim field. `per-guarantee-outcome` records, for every guarantee in the effective version, which rule enforced it and on what evidence — the trace's granularity is per guarantee, with rule mechanics nested beneath it. This is the audit shape that makes "guarantees become policy rules" inspectable: security can ask "under which rule was g1 enforced for this deployment, on what evidence," and the certificate answers.

**Evidence object, snapshot, evaluator.** Adopted from the classify-and-auto-approve proposal with the same demands — provenance classes `{author-supplied, pipeline-derived, pipeline-observed}`, snapshot-only evaluation, total determinism, content-addressed evaluator — because those mechanisms are not direction-specific; they are the shared price of the framing's audit bar ("reproducibility, not a log line"). The evaluator's output differs in semantics only: `qualified | not-qualified`, where `qualified` asserts *rules enforced*, not *risk bounded*.

**Objection record.** Appended to a certificate by any pipeline-identified human: `{objector, reason, sealed-until, resolution, disposition}`. The objector is named in the record (abuse without accountability is rejected in Alternatives), but the name is sealed from the deployer until disposition, revealed to security unconditionally — retaliation-deterrence without anonymity. `reason` is free text by design: the channel exists precisely to capture judgment that does not reduce to rules.

**Divergence and encodability events.** A shadow-phase or live-phase human denial/rework over a qualified deployment yields a divergence record, which a human review classifies `encodable → policy gap (patch rule, matrix update)` or `non-encodable → residue`. Residue feeds the encodability ratio — the direction's standing viability metric. This classification work is reviewer time from the fixed pool; it is budgeted (O3).

**Guarantee-violation event.** An incident, audit finding, or objection resolution that establishes a guarantee was violated despite `enforced` status. This is *not* a policy bug of the patchable kind — it is evidence against the enumeration or the compilation, and it triggers a **representational review**: re-opening the guarantee list and the matrix, with security in the chair. The distinction between "the rule was wrong" and "the guarantee wasn't there or wasn't encoded" is first-class; the latter is the direction's named failure mode (F1) made visible.

**Never-self-qualify set.** Extended from the earlier proposal: the evaluator, policy corpus, guarantee registry, matrix, objection channel, certificate schema, ledger, and pipeline control code are structurally excluded from qualification, checked by change composition. The guarantee registry and matrix are *new* additions — under this direction they are the control above the control, and their changes must be more heavily governed, not equally governed (S5).

### Architecture

Seven components across the same two trust domains as in the earlier proposal, with four structural additions:

**Team-owned.** *(1) Evidence ingestion* — adopted. *(2) The guarantee registry and ratification flow* — the store of guarantee versions, signatures, and provenance; write access restricted to the guarantee body's attested identities; append-only, supersession by new version only. *(3) The policy corpus with per-version traceability matrix* — policy changes are PRs requiring owner + security sign-off *plus a matrix update review*; a policy version released with an unsigned matrix or an `uncompiled` row cannot govern qualification. *(4) The deterministic evaluator* — adopted mechanics, different output semantics. *(5) The certificate store* — appended to the shared ledger; scoped-claim schema enforced by construction. *(6) The objection channel and halt integration* — on-path objection capture, sealed naming, resolution workflow, and the controlled halt/revert affordance for objected-and-deployed changes (this makes the framing's unresolved revert design, Q7, load-bearing; see I7). *(7) The measurement loop, extended* — shadow divergences, the encodability tracker, the GVR monitor (guarantee-violation rate against the security-set threshold), drift and cadence alarms, and the representational audit schedule.

**Platform-owned.** *(8) The mode controller* — and here the platform dependency is structurally larger than the earlier proposal's. Their controller *inserts* a stage before the existing approval stage, which it can satisfy. This direction's controller must **bypass the approval stage entirely for qualified deployments in live mode** — a pipeline-flow change, not an addition. If the platform team's pipeline architecture or their reading of "no side channel" (Q4) rules out an internal bypass, this direction is dead where the earlier proposal might still fit. That fork is a report, not a blocker to design around; it is stated, not resolved.

**Integrity spine, determinism, degradation.** Adopted wholesale — artifact-hash binding ("the thing that was admitted is the thing that ran"), total deterministic policy language with byte-for-byte replay, fail-closed degredation to the human gate. These are common infrastructure of both directions and are not re-derived here.

**Phasing.** *P0 — guarantee elicitation*: ask the approvers (framing Q10 exists because nobody has); ratify guarantee-version 1; no code. This gates everything downstream and is the direction's sharpest dependency (I1). *P1 — recording shadow*: the evaluator runs read-only alongside the human gate; zero enforcement surface; lightest platform ask. *P2 — measured shadow*: disposition capture, divergence measurement, encodability classification; the zero-divergence streak accumulates per policy version, each substantive revision resetting the clock; the encodability ratio is published to security. *P3 — graduated cutover, gated*: per change class, smallest first, each expansion a human-approved policy change with a matrix update, and only when **all** of: guarantee-version 1 ratified and closed; matrix signed; zero divergences over per-class volume and duration; zero guarantee-violation events over a longer window; encodability ratio within security-set bounds; objection channel live with halt integrated; representational audit staffed and scheduled; policy owner and security capacity actually assigned. Live mode begins on the smallest class and expands; a guarantee-violation event or an encodability breach revokes the newest expansion, not silently.

### User workflows

**Deployer.** Push → pipeline → evaluation. Qualified: the deployment proceeds; the UI shows the certificate — which guarantees were enforced, under which rules — and the objection affordance. The word "approved" never appears for this path; the terminology migration (I5) makes that real in the UI, not just the schema. Not-qualified: unchanged human gate, with the per-rule failure trace as pre-review feedback (Q8: yes, the policy and trace are readable; gameability is fought at evidence provenance, not opacity — same position as the earlier proposal, agreed and adopted).

**Reviewer.** The non-qualifying queue, unchanged in ceremony (F6 argues the ceremony must be deliberately preserved), plus three new items: sampled re-reviews (r2), objection resolutions (r3), and encodability classifications of divergences. The framing accepted the high-risk cohort may get slower; this design does not promise otherwise. It promises the same upper bound the earlier proposal did: the qualified path never adds to the queue.

**Policy owner.** Heavier than the earlier proposal's version of the role: every policy change is a PR requiring owner + security sign-off *and* a matrix update review (S5). The owner is the accountable human on every qualified deployment under their version — and the scope of that accountability is unbounded in volume and duration (F2). The role is real work; it is currently unassigned (Q9); live mode cannot start without it.

**Guarantee body** (a role this design surfaces rather than invents). The approvers, formalized: they own the guarantee list, ratify versions, and answer the elicitation questions. Today this role exists implicitly and unaccountably — nobody can say what the gate actually checks ("the baseline control's detection rate is currently unmeasurable"). Phase 0 is the moment that body becomes named and accountable.

**Security.** Four standing surfaces, not two: the ledger/replay tooling; the measurement reports (GVR, divergences, encodability, drift, objections); *the representational audit* — a standing, scheduled human review of whether the guarantee-version and its compilation still mean what the org's guarantees mean; and guarantee-version ratification co-chair. The audit is the direction's answer to its own central weakness (S1), and it consumes security capacity the framing records as unsecured. If that capacity cannot be staffed, live mode cannot be licensed — the design states this as a precondition, not an afterthought.

**Incident responder.** An implicated qualified deployment triggers mandatory human review (r4) *and* a representational review: was this a rule that was wrong, or a guarantee that was missing or mis-compiled? The objection channel is the halt affordance for a change believed dangerous after admission; its halt/revert semantics inherit the framing's emergency-revert gap (Q7).

### Failure modes

**F1. Representation failure — the guarantee set is incomplete or mis-compiled.** The framing's named failure for this direction: "policy gaps are not recorded errors." The system believes a guarantee is enforced; it is not; the certificate is *clean and false*. This is worse than the earlier proposal's misclassification because the record's central claim is falsified, not merely a judgment's accuracy. Mitigations: P0 elicitation; the signed matrix; the standing representational audit; divergence-classified-as-encodable gaps; and **guarantee-violation events as triggers for representational review, not rule patches**. Residual: incompleteness of the enumeration is unprovable, and nothing in any record can reveal a guarantee nobody has articulated. This residual is S1's subject; the design does not pretend to remove it.

**F2. Accountability diffusion under no-decision semantics.** Nobody said "go." The two named humans — deployer and policy owner — are attached to an admission by rule, not a decision-act. The deployer's accountability is defensible (they knowingly pushed under a readable policy); the policy owner's is the vulnerable one: unbounded deployments, unbounded duration, no per-change contact. Mitigations: per-deployment naming (the record names the owner individually each time); version ownership windows; per-class sub-ownership. Residual: accountability as written is satisfied; accountability as *practiced* may be nominal. This is the direction's deepest vulnerability on sponsor-guarantee g3, and it cannot be engineered away — "nobody decided" is the premise, not an accident.

**F3. Guarantee decay.** The org's real guarantees drift (new failure modes emerge; what "looked at" meant in 2026 evolves). The policy encodes the old list; the certificates remain truthfully scoped — and therefore quietly irrelevant. Mitigation: guarantee-version expiry (a version has a max lifetime; lapse fails qualification), scheduled re-elicitation, and the representational audit's explicit drift question.

**F4. Qualification creep via governance bypass.** The boundary is the policy; the meta-rule is that no policy change is possible without matrix review and sign-off. If that meta-rule is eroded — a "minor" rule tweak, a threshold nudge shipped without the matrix ceremony, exactly the pattern Idea 1's threshold-dial analysis predicts — the guarantee encoding changes without the standing human review the encoding exists to provide. Mitigation: every version change, however small, carries the full matrix ceremony; no fast path exists; replay-regression over the historical corpus gates evaluator releases (adopted).

**F5. The objection channel becomes rubber, or toothless, or a side channel.** Three deaths for the one human-brake the design has: objections resolving trivially (rubber), objections with no enforcement affordance (toothless — a human warning about a deployed qualified change that goes nowhere), or objections that route around the pipeline to get effect (side channel — the framing: "no side channel… there is no side channel" violated at the point of maximum need). Mitigation: on-path objection records, forced review with SLA, halt integration (I7), rate alarms, disposition records, sealed naming. Residual: an objection after deployment cannot restore pre-deploy prevention by construction; the design bounds the detection window, not the prevention window.

**F6. Ceremony erosion of the residual human gate.** The qualified path is the pipeline's new mainstream; the non-qualifying path is "the rest." If the vocabulary and ceremony of human approval erode for the residual queue, the one remaining per-change human decision in the system compounds its rubber-stamping problem. Mitigation: the non-qualifying flow is changed by nothing in this design, and policy explicitly forbids touching its ceremony in the same workstream; the framing's attention-quality thesis does the rest.

**F7. Conservative-policy stall.** Security tunes the residual human queue tightly (their prerogative); qualification shrinks; the direction's entire velocity win is spent on the non-qualifying queue's growth. The framing accepted slower high-risk cohort, not slower routine cohort. Mitigation: the qualifying boundary is tuned against observed volume distribution — the design goal is "the routine bulk qualifies," and conservative tuning is a governance-level decision with the velocity consequence made explicit, not a quiet dial.

**F8. Guarantee-version bump vs. in-flight deployments.** When a new guarantee is ratified (the predictable post-incident outcome), guarantee-version bumps and every in-flight deployment is re-evaluated under the successor version — deterministic replay makes this cheap, and a now-failing in-flight deployment fails closed before shipping. Shipped certificates remain truthfully scoped to the version they were decided under; incident review may re-examine them under the new guarantee. This is a standing operation, budgeted (O1), not a surprise.

**F9. The shadow oracle is weak, and the encodability judgment is itself human.** Adoption of the earlier proposal's F10: shadow parity is agreement with a rubber-stamping oracle; the cutover criterion is a zero-divergence streak, deliberately demonstrating too much to pass. This direction adds a second-order weakness: the encodability classification of divergences is a human judgment, and *non-encodable* may mean "the reviewer didn't try hard or the policy language is weak," not "human judgment is irreducibly non-mechanical." Mitigation: encodability classifications are dispute-able records with named classifiers; the ratio carries confidence bounds; the representational audit samples disagreement.

**F10. The halt path (Q7) fails, taking the objection channel with it.** The objection channel's enforcement affordance — halt/revert for admitted changes — inherits the framing's unresolved emergency-revert design ("no design exists"). Built wrong, it becomes the side channel the constraints forbid; unbuilt, F5's toothless variant wins. This is the direction's loudest dependency outside security: an objection channel without a designed halt is a suggestion box, and a suggestion box is not guarantee (a)'s compiled form.

**F11. Scoped-claim misreading.** A document reviewer, auditor, or future engineer reads "all guarantees enforced" in a certificate and — the schema cannot prevent this — hears "the org's guarantees were enforced." The scoping is in the schema, not in the reading. Mitigation: the claim string is constructed with the guarantee-version embedded; display layers render it in full; audit training. Residual: this is a documentation failure the design names rather than assumes away — the direction's records are safe against forgery, not against misunderstanding.

### Operational complexity

**The cost model is the headline.** Per-change human cost for the qualified class rounds to zero; the standing human costs become O(guarantee churn + policy churn + objection volume) instead of O(deployment volume). That is the operational rationale of the direction, and its failure condition is equally crisp: guarantee churn. Every incident that surfaces a new guarantee, every re-elicitation, every version bump triggers representational work — and incidents are roughly O(volume). If the org's guarantee list stabilizes, the design wins on the asymptotic; if it churns, the asymptotic flattens.

**New standing costs, all human, three unstaffed.** Representational audits (security), matrix sign-off per policy change (policy owner + security), objection resolution (reviewer pool), encodability classification (reviewer pool), elicitation sessions (the guarantee body — the approvers, whose time is the fixed capacity the framing says "does not scale with deployment volume"). The framing records security's inspection capacity "has also not been secured." This direction is the *heaviest* consumer of security-team time among the readings: it moves human judgment from the reviewer pool to the security/governance pool — a fixed pool the framing says is already the binding constraint. That is a real operational weakness, stated by the design that carries it, not by its critics.

**Two decision semantics on one pipeline.** Runbooks, dashboards, incident tooling, and audit consumers must distinguish qualified (rule-admitted, two accountable humans, scoped claim) from approved (human decision, one signer). Incident review semantics differ per class (qualified implication → representational review; human-approved implication → normal review). The earlier proposal's single-semantics pipeline — machine-approves-low-risk vs. human-approves-everything-else, one verb — is operationally simpler, and this design concedes that plainly in the disagreements section rather than papering over it.

**Objection-channel staffing competes with the queue.** Objection resolution draws from the same fixed reviewer pool as the non-qualifying queue (F5/F7 already strain it). The design requires an explicit objection-rework budget separated at allocation time; otherwise objections silently starve ordinary review, or ordinary review starves objections, and one of F5's deaths follows. Same logic, adopted: the sampled re-review tax must be carved explicitly before cutover.

**Degradation behavior remains monotonically simple.** Every failure resolves to fewer qualifications, more human queue — "auto-admission unavailable → manual queue, same as before." The ledger and replay standing burden (adopted): growing corpus, retention policy, a batch replay job needing an owner. The measurement loop's self-audit (adopted): an unmeasured measurement system would quietly invalidate the GVR that gates everything downstream.

**Terminology migration is a program, not a rename.** "Approved" lives in pipeline UI, chatops, dashboards, team docs, audit tooling. Phase 3 includes migrating the qualified path to "qualified/certificate/admitted" vocabularies — half-measures produce UX contradicting the records, which is F11's breeding ground. This is mundane, expensive in touches, and mandatory; it is listed here so nobody discovers it in cutover week.

### Security implications

**S1. Completeness is the security boundary, and it is unprovable — the direction's irreducible residual, stated by its designer.** The org constraint is "any automated approval must be inspectable after the fact by the security team." Inspection after the fact can verify *the evaluation ran as claimed* — replay makes that mechanical. Inspection cannot reveal the guarantee that was never enumerated: no record records the missing question. The framing's "silent by construction" is not a bug to fix; it is the direction's security *shape*. The mitigations — elicitation, representational audit, divergence-driven reopening, incident-driven reopening — are governance, and governance can be gamed, delayed, or understaffed in ways machinery cannot. I will not present this direction as safe-by-construction where it is safe-by-hypothesis: the design lives or dies on security's explicit acceptance of **completeness-as-governed-hypothesis** for the qualifying classes. That is the deepest reading of Q1, and it is the license this direction needs — a strictly stronger license than the earlier proposal requires (misclassification is an answer the system gives; an unencoded guarantee is a question the system never asks).

**S2. Scoped claims are the anti-lie device, and they must survive contact with governance.** A certificate asserts "all guarantees in guarantee-version G were enforced" — a sentence that is checkable by replay and true even when the org's real guarantees have moved on (F3). The schema makes absolutely-asserting claims unconstructable. Retroactive guarantee-version bumps re-evaluate in-flight deployments (F8) and re-examine shipped ones under the new guarantee — but shipped certificates are never rewritten; their truth stays scoped. This is what distinguishes honest scoping from cover: the record's modesty is structural, not rhetorical.

**S3. Accountability is only as strong as the security team's accountability model — and this direction has no backup model.** The sponsor's D14 default names deployer plus policy owner. That satisfies a *process-based* accountability model: the system's operation is owned, per deployment, by named humans. It does not satisfy an *act-based* model: no human actuated "this deployment goes." If security's terminology for "deployment decision" (Q5 — "the final definition is the security team's terminology to own") turns out to be act-based, this direction is dead for the qualified class, and no design refinement resurrects it; "nobody decided" is the premise. I state this as a property, not a risk to mitigate: the direction's auditability is maximal for process, minimal for act — the inverse profile of the human gate, and of the earlier proposal, whose machine-approves framing carries at least a synthetic decision-act.

**S4. The objection channel is the one human-brake, and it deserves adversarial review.** It must accept objections on weak evidence — humans often cannot articulate why a change feels dangerous; that is precisely the non-encodable judgment the policy cannot capture (r3 exists because F1 is real). Low evidence requirements make the channel abusable: objection floods as harassment or queue-DoS, objection misclassification as covert rubber-stamping. The design's countermeasures are named objectors with sealed naming, security-set rate thresholds with escalation, disposition records for every objection, and mandatory security review of objected-and-qualified resolutions. The halt affordance (F10) is part of the channel's security posture: an objection channel without halt is a security theater *measured*, and that measurement is the channel's own GVR input.

**S5. Policy governance under guarantee semantics is heavier — and must be.** A policy change is not "the classifier is tuned"; it is "the guarantees' enforcement is redefined." Two controls, both human: per-change sign-off (owner + security), and per-change traceability-matrix review. Above ordinary policy changes sits the **guarantee-version bump** — the highest-authority act in the system, requiring multiple named signatures and structurally unavailable to automation. An attacker who can ratify a guarantee-version bump has removed a guarantee from the set future certificates claim to enforce, remotely approving themselves for everything downstream. The never-self-qualify set (the guarantee registry and matrix included) plus multi-signature ratification is the standing form of the framing's rollout self-application decision (D7), generalized.

**S6. The GVR threshold is a weaker measurement than the earlier proposal's FNR threshold — state the comparison.** A "false negative" there means a dangerous change misclassified. Here it means *a guarantee violated while the system recorded it enforced* — an event that is invisible until an incident, an objection resolution, or the representational audit surfaces it. The shadow loop can measure divergences (denied-by-human-but-qualified), and the design adopts the earlier proposal's zero-divergence streak — but the violation-without-any-denial class ("the change that sailed through both the policy and the rubber-stamping human," their F10) is as invisible here as there, and *more* damaging here, because the direction's central claim is enforcement. The cutover criterion therefore adds the requirement of zero guarantee-violation events over a longer window, and names the residual: a violation that never surfaces is indistinguishable from a guarantee that never needed encoding.

**S7. Adopted security properties.** Provenance classes with author-supplied evidence unable to satisfy safety predicates; secrets-in-records resolution (policy reads derived structure only; raw artifacts retained under access control); identity attestation as the accountability foundation (an attestation-weak platform team inherits the weakness — a platform dependency to state in the negotiation, not to paper over); ledger write-restriction and append-only semantics; the measurement loop's independence from the measured system.

### Implementation constraints

**I1. Guarantee elicitation is Phase 0, an org work item with no code, and it gates everything.** Framing Q10: the guarantee list's completeness is unverified because "the approvers have not been asked." The earlier proposal can build and shadow without touching the guarantee list — its classifier asserts risk, not guarantee enforcement. This direction cannot even *define the claim semantics* of its certificate without a rated guarantee-version: the scoped claim references it. If the approvers cannot articulate their judgment — and the framing's rubber-stamping premise suggests exactly that — the direction stalls before a line of evaluator code matters. The dependency order is hard: elicitation → ratification → matrix → shadow → cutover.

**I2. The platform change is structurally larger: bypass, not insertion.** The earlier proposal's mode controller inserts before the existing approval stage and satisfies it. This direction must bypass the approval stage for qualified deployments in live mode — a flow change to the shared pipeline, implicating Q4's "no side channel" interpretation directly, and requiring the platform team's change process (Q11) on the sharpest possible reading. If the platform team rules the approval stage is the pipeline's single actuator and non-bypassable, this direction is dead where the earlier proposal survives. The phasing (P1 read-only, P2 disposition capture, P3 bypass) is ordered the same way for the same reason: maximum construction before maximum negotiation.

**I3. Schema work for scoped claims is a first-class deliverable.** The constructed-claim string, the guarantee-version reference, the matrix reference, the per-guarantee outcome map — this schema is the direction's audit integrity, and it is not retrofit-able after records exist. It, the evaluator, and the replay tooling ship from the first commit with the same determinism discipline the earlier proposal demands; there is no "lite" schema phase.

**I4. Shared mechanics adopted, with credit:** snapshot-only deterministic evaluation, evidence provenance classes, artifact-hash integrity spine, append-only ledger, replay-regression gating evaluator releases. These are direction-independent requirements of the framing's audit bar and are not re-litigated here.

**I5. Terminology migration is an implementation constraint.** The pipeline UI, dashboards, and documentation must stop saying "approved" for the qualified path by cutover; records and UX that contradict each other are F11's machine. This work belongs on the schedule, not in a docs ticket.

**I6. Open dependencies, carried not silently resolved.** Q2 (criteria values), Q6 (GVR threshold), Q9 (policy owner), Q10 (guarantee list) are prerequisites inherited from the framing. This direction adds two of its own: **security inspection capacity** for representational audits and guarantee ratification — capacity the framing records as unsecured — and **reviewer hours for encodability classification and objection resolution**, carved from the fixed pool before cutover. Live mode requires all of them assigned; shadow mode requires none.

**I7. The objection channel cannot be completed in isolation.** Its halt affordance builds on the framing's emergency-revert design, which "does not exist" (Q7). The implementable slice is: objection records and resolution workflow now; halt/revert semantics as a controlled, audited re-route designed jointly with the platform team's revert-path work. Shipping the channel without the halt is shipping F5's toothless variant and calling it done; this constraint forbids that sequencing.

### Alternatives considered

**A1. Maximal elimination — hard-block everything non-qualifying, so no approval exists anywhere.** Rejected. It converts the policy into a *denylist as the sole control* on the only path to production: the high-risk cohort the framing excludes from this problem would have no route at all, and the framing's baseline gate — whose detection rate is unmeasured, not zero — would be discarded before it was measured. This variant turns the direction into a weapon against the safety priority; the scoped elimination (qualified only) is the entire defensible form.

**A2. Rename-only — keep approval everywhere, relabel it "policy enforcement."** Rejected. It changes no semantics for the non-qualifying path and burdens the qualified path with a concept the direction explicitly removes. Audit consequences: two vocabularies in one record corpus with no semantic difference — strictly worse confusion than either honest design.

**A3. Literal preservation of g1 — every qualified deployment requires a human "look" token.** Rejected. A required per-change human look is per-change human review with extra steps; it re-imports the fixed-capacity bottleneck, produces rubber tokens at scale, and kills the velocity outcome the direction exists to deliver. It is the direction's temptation to hedge, and the design resists it: g1 is modified or the direction is abandoned, not both.

**A4. Compilation without a traceability matrix — rely on security's review of the policy text.** Rejected. Unverifiable per record: a certificate could only gesture at "guarantees enforced" rather than name the rule-to-guarantee mapping, and replay could not re-check the compilation. The matrix is the audit affordance that makes "guarantees become policy rules" inspectable after the fact, which is the org's hard constraint.

**A5. Score-threshold qualification.** Rejected for the same reasons the earlier proposal rejected it (compensation across axes; a tunable dial that feels operational rather than governance-grade). Guarantees compound conjunctively: every guarantee must be enforced, no score can buy off an unenforced one.

**A6. No objection channel — pure rule-governedness.** Rejected. The modified g1 would then have no per-change human form at all — only per-rule and incident-reactive — which dilutes the guarantee past the point the framing allows ("a human has looked at every change" surviving "in observable form"). The channel is the reactive per-change form; without it the design is a one-way door with no human brake between a qualified deployment and production, and the direction's honesty about modifying g1 would be a euphemism for deleting it.

**A7. Anonymous objections.** Rejected. Anonymity without accountability invites abuse of the one human-brake (S4); named-with-sealed-disclosure keeps accountability and reduces retaliation risk. Free-text reasons are kept — requiring objectors to satisfy a rule-based burden of proof would filter out exactly the non-encodable judgment the channel exists to capture.

### Disagreements with earlier proposals

The classify-and-auto-approve proposal is the only predecessor, and it is a strong one. Its mechanics are largely adopted here — but its central semantic choice is the one this direction exists to oppose, and that opposition is recorded plainly rather than converged away.

**D1. On whether "auto-approval" is approval.** Their design preserves the concept with a machine actor; their A7 dismisses no-decision semantics as manufacturing "unauditable non-decisions." Their A7 rejects the *degenerate* Idea-2 variant — the sponsor's D14 rejects exactly that variant too, and this direction includes that rejection. Where we actually part: I hold that preserving the approval container while replacing its occupant is *less honest* than removing the container. Their record claims a machine approved the change as low-risk — a safety assertion about a specific change, of the kind the framing's unmeasurable-baseline problem undermines most. This direction's record claims the guarantees in force were enforced — a control assertion about the process, checkable by replay and scoped to a named guarantee-version. Both claims need security's license (Q1); they are different licenses, and they do not transfer between the designs. I assert security might accept one and reject the other; the designs' risk profiles differ accordingly.

**D2. On the semantic home of the screening predicates.** Their conjunction rules — composition, blast radius, detection coverage — are posed as the classifier's affirmative low-risk claim. I relocate effectively identical predicate content under the modified g1 as the compiled residue of "a human has looked," recorded in the traceability matrix. Operational content: near-identical. Governance consequences: not — under their home a predicate change is classifier tuning; under mine it is a guarantee-encoding change requiring matrix update and representational review. Their home is operationally cheaper; mine is what "guarantees become policy rules" means taken literally, and this direction pays the cost rather than evading the sentence.

**D3. On where the residual human presence lives.** Their compensation for lost per-change review is sampled re-review as *measurement* of classifier error. Mine is sampled re-review as *the guarantee (g1) operating in sampled form*, plus a reactive objection channel their design lacks — their OVERRIDE forces an approval through; it is not a human veto on a live qualified deployment. The difference falls out of the semantics: an override exists because approval still exists as a concept; an objection channel must exist because nothing in the qualified path can be overridden — there is nothing to override. My design has a human-present reactive control theirs lacks; theirs has a forcing control mine lacks (under mine, force-approval is simply the human gate's ordinary operation). Each is coherent with its own semantics; neither is superior in the abstract.

**D4. Operational simplicity — I concede a real loss.** Their pipeline has one decision verb, two actors. Mine has two verbs and two accountability shapes — and every downstream consumer (runbooks, incident tooling, audit training) inherits the distinction. Removing the concept costs more operationally than re-casting it, and this is a genuine advantage of their design over mine, conceded without argument.

**D5. On what the shadow period demonstrates.** Their cutover criterion — a zero-divergence streak over per-class volume and duration — demonstrates the classifier never disagrees with a human in the dangerous direction. That is necessary here too, and adopted. But their shadow asks only "did the machine match the human"; this direction's shadow must also ask **"was the human's judgment encodable,"** and their design contains no instrument that answers it. The encodability tracker is therefore my strongest addition, and its absence from their design is my strongest methodological objection: a direction premised on compiling human judgment (their own policy is a written compilation of it) should measure whether the compilation is succeeding, and classes its own failure signal. My design's shadow loop is a falsification instrument for the premise; theirs is a tuning loop for the classifier. On this we diverge sharply and I do not expect convergence: they may reasonably argue the shadow period's job is tuning, not philosophy. I hold that under the efficiency reading, the philosophy *is* the acceptance test.

**D6. On the security-team capacity burden — my direction is the heavier consumer.** Their design's standing security demand is thresholds, reports, and override review. Mine compounds it: representational audits, matrix sign-offs, guarantee-version ratification, and objection dispositions are all security-staffed, from a pool the framing records as unsecured. This is a comparative weakness of my own direction: where capacity is the binding constraint, the direction that relocates human judgment *into* the security team is the one most exposed to not being staffed. I record this against myself; their design is lighter here and should keep that advantage in the compaction's trade-off ledger, not have it erased by convergence pressure.

## Design proposal — differentiated-human-review

### Approach

This design holds the direction's sentence as an invariant: **the approval is a human decision for every deployment, and the machine never makes, fakes, or front-runs that decision.** What gets re-engineered is everything around it — classification, presentation, routing, batching, and measurement — so that the fixed reviewer capacity the constraints assume ("reviewer capacity is fixed and does not scale with deployment volume") lands on the changes that need human judgment, and routine-shaped changes stop waiting behind judgment-shaped ones. This is the framing's Reading 4 ("faster informed human decisions via tiering, routing, batching, better pre-review evidence — without touching the approval concept. Compatible with every org constraint as written") plus the defensible half of Idea 3 — assistance — with the indefensible half — recommendation of outcomes or default-approval windows — rejected (Alternatives A1, A2).

Core commitments, stated so later agents can challenge them precisely:

1. **No deployment decision is automated, by mechanism or by creep.** The disqualifying line is bright: any mechanism that lets a deployment proceed without a recorded human disposition for *that deployment* is a different design. Batch signatures, "no objection within N minutes," lapse-implies-approve — all excluded. The direction's last clause is load-bearing: "a named accountable human still signs off every deployment." One change, one human decision, one named sign-off, one audit record.

2. **Classification is advice about attention, never a claim about safety.** The tier assessment says: *this change is routine-shaped; a reviewer can confirm it in minutes* — or *this change touches judgment territory; here is where*. It never asserts "low-risk" or "dangerous," because both are claims about the deployment's fate, and the deployment's fate has exactly one arbiter: the human. The framing's Q3 ("the evidence bar differs materially between [a machine classifier and a human deciding quickly]") is exploited, not evaded: this is the *human-deciding-quickly* consumer, which is precisely the consumer the framing's D15 left explicitly unmade. Because classification errors here are attention-allocation errors, not deployment-decision errors, the classifier can ship under today's rules with **no answer to Q1 required**. That is the design's license, and it is claimed deliberately.

3. **The decision unit is the deployment, always.** Batching is a presentation and scheduling mechanism, never a decision mechanism. This is the design's answer to the guarantee question that bends every other direction: guarantees (a) — "a human has looked at every change" — and (b) — "a recorded sign-off with a name" — survive **untouched, in per-change form**. No modification, no relocation, no sampled substitute. The framing records the sponsor can name only two guarantees plus the accountability addition (D3); this design preserves all three without asking which ones may be renegotiated.

4. **Assistance, not recommendation — a sub-choice of the direction, stated deliberately.** The review brief presents what the change is (mechanically derived), what is verified and by whom, what is not verified, and what the policy says the reviewer's attention should be. It does not recommend an outcome, because a verdict-shape recommendation is the brief's information content minus the reasoning plus an anchoring term (Alternatives A1). "Approve" and "deny" remain human acts; the system performs neither, even as advice.

5. **The gate is untouched; this design is an overlay in front of it.** The pipeline's approval event is the same event, made by the same humans, recorded the same way — plus instrumentation. Consequently Q1 (security's position on automation), Q5 (the auditable unit under policy-only deployments), and Q6 (the false-negative threshold) never gate this design, and Q4 ("no side channel" interpretation for class-differentiated gates) **never arises** — there are no class-differentiated gates because there are no class-differentiated decisions. The same gate serves every class; what differs is the evidence in front of it.

6. **The human gate remains the sole control, so this design has no dangerous-false-negative class of its own.** A tiering error wastes or misallocates attention; it cannot release a change. The design's worst operational failure is the status quo — today's gate with today's queue. It cannot produce an open gate, and unlike the automation designs it cannot degrade the gate's *availability* either (Operational complexity).

7. **Re-engineered attention becomes measurable, auditable signal.** Every review leaves a record of what the human was shown (brief version, content-addressed), what attention level policy allotted, what they did with it (disposition, time, tier disagreement), and a named sign-off. This delivers the framing's D5 baseline instrumentation — denials, reworks, and incident attribution made queryable — *plus a review-context dimension no other direction records for human decisions*. Attention quality, the framing's own success metric ("attention quality, not hours"), becomes an observable, not an assertion.

8. **What this design plays as, and its honest ceiling.** It is the information reading with the control-quality reading's assistance absorbed. It optimizes the framing's success metric directly, and buys routine-change latency through queue segmentation and per-change overhead compression — not through decision elimination. Its ceiling is the framing's own admission about Reading 4: "may not reduce hours much." The per-change human look cannot be engineered below a floor of roughly a minute-scale confirmation. This design states that floor and does not pretend past it (F9).

**Assumptions and non-resolutions.** The tier predicate *values* (which services, what diagnostic coverage, what class of composition is routine-shaped) remain unowned — framing Q2 is inherited, not resolved; but the consequence of unresolved values here is misallocated attention, so live operation can proceed with conservative defaults and a temporary policy owner where the automation designs could not start at all. Q10 (guarantee-list completeness) remains open and **is not needed**: this design compiles no guarantees into rules, so nothing depends on the enumeration being complete. The one dependency unique to this design replaces the security conversation as the first step: the approvers must be asked — not "what could be automated," but "what makes your review expensive" (Implementation I2).

### System primitives

**Tier assessment.** `{tier ∈ {R, J}, predicate-outcomes[], trace, policy-version, artifact-hash}`, produced by a deterministic evaluator (adopted from the classify-and-auto-approve proposal, credited below). **R** — routine-shaped: every conjunctive predicate affirms on pipeline-derived or pipeline-observed evidence. **J** — judgment: any predicate fails, is unverifiable, or touches the judgment domain. The tier is an attention recommendation with a full trace of how it was arrived at; the trace's consumer is the *reviewer*, who sees which predicates failed and on what evidence.

**Tiering policy.** Machine-checkable rules that encode **how much attention approval requires, not whether approval is required** — the org context defines policy as "machine-checkable rules that encode when approval is required"; this design's policy is a complement to that definition, never a replacement of it. It governs: the conjunctive predicate set (adopted below), tier mapping, batching cohort rules (which R items may be presented together), routing rules, J-queue escalation thresholds, and a **never-R set** — tiering-system code, policy corpus, pipeline-control and security-critical configuration are structurally excluded from R. Versioned, content-addressed, changes approved separately with a named owner (framing D10, applied), replay-preview over the historical corpus on change. The policy asserts *nothing about safety* — its failure mode is misallocated attention, which is why its governance is lighter than the gate-policy governance the automation designs require (Security S6).

**The conjunctive predicate set — adopted with two deletions.** From the classify-and-auto-approve proposal: `artifact-integrity`, `composition-bounded` (mechanically derived from the diff; declared type never a policy input), `blast-radius-bounded`, `failure-detectable`, `tests-affirmative`, `no-special-infra` (feature flags, traffic routing, security settings, credentials, migrations, infrastructure code force J), `history-clean`. **Deleted:** `identity-attested` — identity is not an attention-allocation input; the human gate handles who signs as it does today. **Deleted:** `mode-live` — inapplicable; there is no cutover mode. **Kept at the same bar:** for safety-relevant predicates, author-supplied evidence cannot satisfy the predicate; a fact only the author asserts forces J, where the unverified assertion is displayed for exactly the human attention it should attract. The bars differ from the gate designs in *consequence*, not in structure — the provenance discipline is identical, because the brief will be read by the sole control's humans (Security S2).

**Review brief.** The rendered product, content-addressed: (1) what changed, mechanically derived, never the author's summary; (2) the tier and the predicate trace — which rules passed, on what evidence class, which failed and why; (3) the verified/unverified split, provenance displayed per fact — the framing's D13 ("input values *and their source*") rendered for a human; (4) blast-radius and affected-service graph; (5) test evidence; (6) the policy's attention statement — expected review depth, expected duration, and what the policy says the reviewer may treat as recorded vs. must verify; (7) the disposition control — approve / deny / rework / flag-tier / flag-brief, each a per-deployment action.

**Review-context record.** New in this design: `{deployment-id, brief-version, tier, tier-agreement ∈ {none, concur, disputed}, reviewer-time, disposition, reason, sign-off-name, timestamp}` — appended to the existing approval record; the sign-off is the existing sign-off. This is the asset that makes attention auditable: for every deployment, one can ask what the human was shown and what they did with it. It also pins *which brief version* the human acted on, which turns a lying brief from an unprovable allegation into a replayable fact (Safety F2).

**Disposition records.** Adopted as the framing's P0 requirement: deny and rework become first-class, queryable records with reasons — the single most important behavioral intervention in the whole design, because **a recorded denial reason is the label every learned classifier in the automation directions needs and does not have**. Disposition capture is simultaneously the baseline instrumentation (framing D5) and this design's load-bearing behavioral lever.

**Attention metrics.** The success metric in executable form: review-time distributions per tier; tier-disagreement rate; brief-flag rate (reviewers flagging brief claims as wrong — the brief-quality signal); denial/rework rates per tier against the measured P0 baseline; queue age per tier; cohort sizes. **There is no false-negative criterion to satisfy**, because there is no machine decision whose false negatives could release a change. The design's acceptance criterion is differentiation plus parity: R review is fast while J review stabilizes or improves, and denial/rework rates per class hold or improve against baseline.

### Architecture

The adoption posture: the earlier proposals' integrity spine — evidence provenance classes, deterministic snapshot-only evaluation with a content-addressed evaluator, artifact-hash binding, append-only stores — is required by the framing's audit bar ("reproducibility, not a log line") regardless of direction, and is adopted wholesale with credit, not re-derived. The architecture difference is what the evaluation *feeds*: a screen, not a gate.

**Team-owned.** *(1) Evidence ingestion* — adopted mechanics; the one risk-bearing component, since its output is shown to the sole control's humans (S2). *(2) Tier assessment engine* — pure deterministic function, snapshot-only, byte-reproducible; replay exists here not for security's re-inspection of decisions but for brief-accuracy auditing and policy regression previews. *(3) Review-context store* — append-only, write-restricted to the review UI's disposition hook; no mutation. *(4) Presentation layer* — the tiered queues, cohort views, and the brief renderer; the policy's per-deployment disposition invariant is enforced here and in the store schema (Implementation I4). *(5) Measurement loop* — attention metrics, disagreement trends, escalation alarms for aging J items, drift alarms on tier distributions. *(6) Policy governance* — PRs with named human approval, replay-preview over the historical corpus on change.

**Platform-owned, explicitly out of this team's build:** *the gate, unchanged.* What *is* needed from the platform team is real but categorically different from the automation designs' asks: disposition-reason capture and a review-context hook in their review UI (small, and P0-instrumentation anyway), brief rendering in the review screen (medium), and tiered/batched queue views (medium-large). All presentational. If the platform team refuses integration, this design degrades to a sidecar mode — briefs rendered in an adjacent tool, reviewer keeps two windows — degraded, worse, but *operational*, with the gate untouched. The other designs have no such degradation floor: their gate machinery is all-or-nothing against the platform team's answer to Q4. I count that as a structural advantage, not a convenience.

**Degradation.** Tier engine down, evidence gap, policy lapse → no brief → the review screen exactly as today. The gate's availability has no dependency on the overlay's availability, which is the reliability story (priority 2) resolved by construction: the sole control's uptime is what it was yesterday.

**Phasing.** *P0* — baseline instrumentation: disposition capture with reasons, review-context hook, incident-attribution linkage (framing D5; shared with every direction). *P1* — recording tiering: the tier engine evaluates and records every deployment from read access; briefs rendered nowhere yet; zero enforcement or presentation surface. *P2* — instrumented presentation rollout: tiered queues, then briefs, then cohorts, each feature-flagged and measured against the P0 baseline (this is D6 — "a shadow period is mandatory" — in the only form that applies to a design with no machine decision: no cutover of any decision-maker occurs, so there is no zero-divergence criterion; the discipline is *measured presentation change with per-feature reversion*, argued in Disagreements D3). *P3* — steady state: policy iteration from disagreement and disposition signals.

### User workflows

**Deployer.** Push → pipeline → build, tests, tier assessment recorded → the same human gate as always. What changes: if the change lands in an R cohort, expected latency is cohort schedule, not queue position behind J work; if J, the deployer's brief shows *which predicates failed and on what evidence* — actionable pre-review feedback, the framing's Q8 made useful. On Q8's transparency question I take the same side as both prior proposals: policy text and the tier trace are always visible to the deployer. Gameability is fought at provenance (declarations are invisible to the predicate set), not opacity — and the stakes are one category lower than in the automation designs, because the gameable payoff here is a *shorter line*, not a *bypassed human*. A malicious deployer who shapes a change to R still faces exactly the human who would have seen it anyway, now holding the mechanically derived summary; the shaping tricks the classifier into recommending cheap attention, not into deciding anything (S3).

**Reviewer — R-session.** The reviewer opens the R queue and takes a cohort: routine-shaped items grouped by shared context (same service, same change class), each rendered as a one-screen brief. The verified predicates show green with sources; the screen's visual weight sits on the *deltas* — what changed since the last release of this service, what is unverified, what the policy says needs a human glance. Motion through the cohort is per-item: each has its own disposition control; the atomic act is the per-deployment disposition (approve/deny/rework/flag), one keystroke after a glance, and each act appends a disposition record with the reviewer's attested identity. Expected per-item time: minutes, policy-stated; cohort cap: policy-set. After the cohort, a summary appears — *N reviewed, X denied, Y reworked, T time spent* — and the summary is a report, not a signature. The signatures are the N per-item records. This is the throughput mechanism, and it is honest about which of the framing's targets it hits: hours per change *do* fall, through amortized context and compressed per-change overhead, while per-change human *decision* remains intact.

**Reviewer — J-session.** The J queue, sorted by escalation with aging items first. The brief leads with the failed predicates — the screen is organized as *why this needs judgment*, then the evidence. No batching of J items: shared non-routine context is the opposite of attention savings. Time is unbounded by policy; this is where the fixed pool goes, and the framing has already accepted the high-risk cohort may get slower. What this design *adds* to that acceptance is a guard the framing did not assume: J-queue starvation that comes from queue neglect, not attention spend, is a managed failure with escalation thresholds (F4) — the accepted slowdown is *because* attention went there, never *because nobody looked*.

**The only new reviewer seconds.** Disposition reasons (P0, shared across directions — new work, and the price of queryable denials), tier flags (one click when the tier seems wrong — this is the classifier's feedback label, produced for free by its consumers, which is the label set the automation directions lack), and brief flags (one click when the brief lies — the brief-quality signal). Notably: **there is no sampled re-review tax.** Every change already receives full human review, so the review *is* the measurement. The classify design taxes the fixed pool with a "deliberate measurement tax"; this design taxes it zero, because its measurement is the control itself.

**Routing/queue manager — a new, honest role.** Owns queue presentation, WIP limits per reviewer, cohort formation, escalation. Rotated, part-time. This is the design's distinctive operational load (Operational complexity): where the machine designs replace process with machinery, this design invests in process, and somebody must be named to run it. Unstaffed, it is F4.

**Policy owner.** Writes tiering-policy changes as PRs with named human approval; accountable for attention allocation under their version. Contrast with the automation designs' policy owner: **no per-deployment accountability attaches**, because nothing decided anything — the owner answers for queue behavior and classification quality, not for deployments. Security co-ownership (framing D10's proposal) is *optional* here, not a precondition: the policy never decides, so its sign-off need not be security's.

**Security.** The role shrinks to reading: disposition records, tier-disagreement rates, brief-audit reports, escalation alarms. No thresholds to own — there is no false-negative rate of a machine decision to calibrate against a baseline (Q6 never fires; the system's only "false negatives" are human errors, exactly as today). The framing records security's inspection capacity "has also not been secured," and both automation designs bill against it (thresholds and override review for one; the other self-reports as "the heaviest consumer of security-team time among the readings"). This design's standing bill to that unsecured pool is approximately zero — and the reports it produces are the homework security needs *for* the Q1 conversation whenever they have it. That is the design's sequencing argument in one sentence.

**Incident responder.** Attribution is now queryable through the disposition chain: deployment → reviewer → brief version → tier → reason. An incident implicates a deployment; the record shows who looked, what they were shown, how long they spent, what they did. This closes the framing's "incident attribution ... not queryable" hole for the human path — the same hole the automation designs' decision ledgers close for machine paths at the cost of a license, and here closed without one.

### Failure modes

**F1. The behavioral bet loses — reviewers still rubber-stamp in the differentiated flow.** The framing lists as unproven the assumption "attention quality serves screening quality." If the rubber stamp is behavioral (incentives, theater), briefs and cohorts make efficient theater. The design's answer is that this failure is *visible*: review times, reason quality, denial rates per tier are measured, and the failing state is a dashboard, not a shrug. And the consequence of this failure is the status quo — not a worse one. If the measured, well-fed human gate catches nothing, that result *falsifies this design* and is precisely the evidence that licenses the automation readings. That is the correct failure path, recorded plainly.

**F2. The brief lies.** Briefs are generated by replayable deterministic machinery, but the evidence layer can be wrong — bad provenance, stale blast-radius graph, a buggy diff derivation. A wrong brief shown to a trusting reviewer produces exactly today's error (a human missed something) with one addition: the review-context record pins the lying brief's version, so the error is attributable and the brief generator is auditable after the fact. The standing brief-audit — sample briefs against their artifacts, track the error rate — is the deliberate cost counterpart of the automation designs' sampled re-review, and it is orders of magnitude cheaper: auditing a brief against an artifact is minutes; re-reviewing a deployment decision is a full review.

**F3. Tier-label bias.** Reviewers over-trust R and under-screen — the classic automation-bias risk, now applied to an advisory label. Mitigations: the tier is *shown with its failed or held predicates* so "R" never arrives as a bare verdict; the R screen is engineered so the review act confirms deltas rather than trusting the label; disagreement is one click and measured; and per-tier outcome statistics are displayed back to the pool (including the reviewer's own denial rate per tier) so complacency has numbers to contradict. The residual — bias that no display removes — is measurable through the same loop and disciplined by the fact that the human still decides; automation bias in this design degrades to *confirmation-level review of something that needed judgment*, not to anything worse than today's review of that same change.

**F4. Routing neglect — J-queue starvation.** Reviewers prefer R cohorts; the J queue ages; the framing's accepted "high-risk cohort may get slower" becomes queue abandonment instead of attention spend. This is a red-line failure, not an accepted trade: routing is presentation, never ownership — any reviewer may take any item; WIP limits span tiers; aging escalates to the queue manager and policy owner. The accepted slowdown is the *time spent reviewing*, never the *time spent unassigned*.

**F5. Batch→decision creep.** The cohort summary tempts a "sign all" gesture; the UI makes per-item disposition the only available verb, the batch view has no spanning action, the summary has no sign-off, and cohort rules are policy — softening them is a policy change with governance, not a UI convenience. The counterweight is spending convenience (priority 4) deliberately: one-keystroke disposition per item so the per-item act costs near zero and social pressure has no efficiency argument to lean on. Priority order applied: convenience is spent to defend the guarantee.

**F6. Tier-boundary rot.** The R boundary decays — service lists go stale, coverage maps drift, policy versions lapse. Adopted mitigations (version expiry, drift alarms, distribution monitoring) now with a cheaper failure: a rotted boundary misallocates attention and *shows up as rising tier-disagreement and review times*, which is exactly the signal the measurement loop watches. The design's rot detector is its own consumers' flags — the same reviewers the gate designs pay separately to observe.

**F7. The tiering system as attack surface.** A compromised evidence or tiering service can poison briefs — flood "routine, all green" — to dilute the sole control's attention. Containment: the tiering service has **no actuator**. Its compromise is a presentation-layer compromise; recovery is reversion to the plain gate by feature flag. Compromised gate machinery in the automation designs is a category of incident this design structurally cannot have.

**F8. Reason fatigue.** Denial/rework reasons degrade to boilerplate and the label set rots. Mitigation: structured reason classes plus free text, empty-reason tracking, reason quality sampled as part of the attention-quality metric. This is the design's quiet maintenance burden — the price of the label stream the whole family of designs consumes.

**F9. The velocity floor disappoints.** Per-reviewer throughput rises, but capacity remains fixed; if volume outgrows it, routine latency returns. The design's exit from this failure is the honest one: the disposition dataset and attention metrics become the baseline and labels the automation readings need for their Q1 conversation and their classifiers — this design is the on-ramp to them, not a competitor that forecloses them (Disagreements D1, D7).

### Operational complexity

**The headline is degradation simplicity.** The overlay down → today's gate. The gate down → today's problem, unchanged. No runbook has to describe what happens to the *control* when the new system misbehaves, because the control never depended on it. Both automation designs carry a richer degradation ladder (mode controller states, evaluator-in-the-decision-path); this design's ladder has one rung and it is the ground floor.

**Built and run — the standing machinery.** Evidence ingestion, tier engine, review-context store, measurement loop, policy governance: this is the *same machinery family* the automation designs bill, at a lower feed pressure — no mode controller, no security-facing one-command-replay tooling as a safety dependency (replay exists, but serves the brief audit, not the security license), no sampled re-review, no cutover ceremony. The genuinely new surface of cost is the **presentation layer and the platform team's review-UI integration** — a UI project under their change process, not a gate project. That is the design's heaviest platform ask, and it is lighter than a mode controller or a bypass by category, not by degree (Disagreements D2).

**The distinctive load is process, and it is billed, not hidden.** Routing rules, WIP limits, cohort formation, reviewer scheduling, escalation — this is standing *management* work with a named owner (queue manager, rotated). The machine designs replace this process with machinery and pay in governance instead (thresholds, representational audits, matrix sign-offs). For a fixed-capacity team, a process investment is reallocatable and a governance tax is not; I count that an operational advantage, but it is the line item most likely to be quietly understaffed, and understaffing is F4.

**The reviewer-hour budget, accounted.** New reviewer seconds: disposition reasons (P0, charged once to every direction since the framing demands the instrumentation regardless), tier flags, brief flags. The measurement tax the classify design bills as "deliberate" and "security-set" — sampled human re-review of machine decisions — does not exist here, because the review is the measurement. The policy-replaces-approval design bills representational audits, matrix sign-offs, encodability classification, and objection resolution against the same fixed pool and the unsecured security capacity; this design's analogous line items are the brief audit (a fraction of one person, mechanical-plus-sampling) and queue management (rotation). It is the only design whose standing human cost *declines* per deployment reviewed rather than adding a parallel review track.

**Rollback is a feature flag, not a migration.** Per-presentation feature, individually revertable; full reversion to today's gate is one deploy of the overlay. Records stay valid — the reviews happened, and append-only context records remain truthful under reversion, which no machine-decision ledger can say after a control-semantics rollback.

**The measurement loop's honesty clause** (adopted from the earlier proposals): the loop itself must be audited so the attention-quality numbers cannot quietly flatter the design. An unmeasured measurement system invalidates the success metric the framing made the *point*. Here the loop's error is an over-confident dashboard, not an over-confident gate — lower stakes, same discipline.

**Coexistence cost with the eventual automation reading.** Because P0/P1 machinery (evidence ingestion, disposition capture, tier engine) is exactly the front half of the classification designs, and the disposition-plus-disagreement records are exactly the labels their learned classifiers need, this direction's sunk cost is the common trunk: if the security conversation later licenses automation, nothing here is thrown away. The reverse is not true — automation machinery would not cleanly convert back to this design.

### Security implications

**S1. The control surface is unchanged.** Human approval audits as today, now with richer records. The org constraint "any automated approval must be inspectable after the fact by the security team" is satisfied vacuously — there is no automated approval. The framing's Q1 gates "the two automation readings"; it does not gate this one, and that is not a loophole, it is the direction's definition. The security conversation can proceed on its own schedule; this design runs *now*.

**S2. The trust boundary moves into the brief — and that is the design's main security property to defend.** The brief is newly load-bearing: evidence shown to the humans operating the sole control. Defenses, all adopted from the earlier proposals' spine: author-supplied evidence cannot satisfy safety-relevant predicates for R (forcing J where a claim is unverifiable); the brief is derived from a deterministic, content-addressed evaluator over provenance-tagged evidence, so any shown brief is replayable; review-context records pin which brief each decision consumed, so a lying brief is attributable; the standing brief audit samples briefs against artifacts. The tiering service's compromise degrades presentation, not the gate; the briefs aggregate change contents, so the store carries access control and secrets hygiene (S4, adopted: predicates read derived structure only; raw diffs retained under access control).

**S3. The manipulator's space shrinks by construction.** Because R assignment requires *mechanical derivation* from the artifact, a deployer hiding a dangerous change must fool both the derivation and the human — and tripping the derivation puts the change in J exactly where the concealment targets. The attacker's remaining play is shaping-to-R for cheaper attention, whose payoff is minutes, and whose detection is the adopted history/cadence predicate plus disagreement monitoring. Modest but real, and I state it without overselling: compared to today's plain-diff review, concealment is slightly harder and its detection is an alarm instead of an after-incident discovery.

**S4. Adopted hygiene.** Provenance classes retained; derived-only rendering; secrets-in-records resolution as in the earlier proposals (policy reads structure, not raw content).

**S5. The never-R set encodes the rollback self-application weakly, and that is the point.** Tiering code, policy corpus, pipeline-control and security-critical configuration are structurally J — the never-self rule in advisory form. But the framing's rollout paradox ("the first deployment of a new approval mechanism is itself a high-risk change") shrinks to near-triviality here: this mechanism does not alter the fate of any future deployment, so its own deployment is a normal-risk change through the unchanged human gate. The paradox was an argument *for* human approval of the replacement mechanism; where nothing is replaced, there is no paradox to resolve.

**S6. Policy governance without the security precondition.** D10's owners, version pinning, and separate approval all apply — but because the policy's failure mode is attention, not gate, security co-ownership is optional, and live operation cannot be stranded on the unsecured security capacity the framing flags. The one governance property that must not erode: every per-deployment disposition is a separate recorded act (Implementation I4), because that single invariant is what keeps guarantees (a) and (b) in per-change form.

**S7. The honest residual, stated by its designer.** This is an attention design, not a capacity design. It cannot make fixed capacity exceed deployment volume. It makes the *decision* to automate — if capacity forces it — a decision made on data this design produces, by the security team this design does not pre-empt. What security does not get is a pre-licensed automation answer; what it gets is the measured human-gate baseline the framing's D5 committed to and the queryable attribution records the framing says do not exist today.

### Implementation constraints

**I1. P0 is shared and non-negotiable** (framing D5): disposition capture with reasons, review-context hook, incident-attribution linkage. It gates every direction including the automations; this design's own machinery builds atop it.

**I2. Reviewer interviews are this design's Phase 0, replacing the security conversation as the gating dependency.** The framing records "the approvers have not been asked" (Q10). The automation directions need the approvers to enumerate guarantees and license ceilings; this design needs them to answer cheaply: *what do you re-derive today that the pipeline already knows; what can you not verify; what makes a queue day expensive.* The output is the attention-allocation policy's first values and the first predicate list. If the approvers cannot or will not answer, the design runs on conservative defaults with an interim policy owner — degraded, not blocked.

**I3. The platform dependency is presentational, and the degradation path exists if refused.** Review-UI integration (brief rendering, tiered queues, cohort views, disposition-reason capture). No gate-semantics change, no Q4 reading required. If refused outright: sidecar tooling adjacent to the platform UI — strictly worse (two windows, no inline disposition capture) but operational, with the gate untouched. No other reading has a fallback position this close to the ground.

**I4. Per-deployment disposition is a schema invariant, not UI discipline.** The review-context store's schema admits one disposition record per deployment-id; the batch view contains no action whose semantics span items; the cohort summary has no sign action. Enforced in the store and the UI both, so no future convenience change can silently convert batching from presentation to decision — that conversion would be a policy change with governance, which is where F5 dies.

**I5. Determinism from the first commit, adopted.** Tier assessment is total, snapshot-only, content-addressed; briefs replayable; review-context records pin brief digests. The bar is the same discipline as the automation designs, but the *consumer* differs: replay here serves brief-audit and policy regression, not security's re-inspection of decisions. The discipline is still non-negotiable, because a non-replayable brief is an unauditable input to the sole control.

**I6. Rollout under current rules, with D6 adapted.** The overlay deploys through the unchanged human gate as a normal-risk change. D6's shadow period is honored in its applicable form — feature-flagged, measured presentation rollout against the P0 baseline, per-feature revert — and its inapplicable form (a zero-divergence cutover criterion) is argued rather than silently dropped (Disagreements D3). Safety parity here is not "the machine caught everything the human caught" but the continuous display of denial/rework rates per class against baseline — the gate's behavior, observed before and after each presentation change.

**I7. Inherited open items, carried not resolved.** Q2 — predicate values unowned: the design runs with conservative defaults and requires a named interim policy owner before P3; missing values fail R allocation, which degrades to a queue-management-only design (still better than today's single FIFO queue, but no throughput win). Q10 — guarantee completeness: explicitly *not* a blocker, because nothing is compiled. The never-R set needs platform-team agreement on what counts as pipeline-control code, which is a list negotiation, not a control negotiation.

### Alternatives considered

**A1. Advisory recommendation — the machine proposes approve/deny upstream of the human decision** (Idea 3's advisory-UI variant). Rejected. A verdict-shaped recommendation is the brief's information content minus the reasoning plus an anchoring term; every fact a recommendation encodes is already in the predicate trace, and the verdict framing is precisely where automation bias does its work. The reviewer's job becomes refuting a machine verdict instead of screening a change. The design's sub-choice (commitment 4) is assistance without recommendation, and it is the load-bearing difference between this direction and Idea 3's diluted form.

**A2. Default-approve-with-a-window** (Idea 3's second variant). Rejected outright. It is automation with a timer — the framing correctly classes it as "a de facto automation [that] must then meet the automation bar of ideas 1/2" — and it manufactures the worst decision record in the solution space: the decision is a non-event ("nobody objected within N minutes"), with either no accountable party or one fabricated for it, which is exactly the "unauditable non-decision" the sponsor's D14 default rejects. It additionally bakes in an availability hazard: a deployment that must not proceed can only be stopped if a human happens to be watching the window. It inherits every Q1, Q5, and Q6 dependency of the automation directions while keeping none of their audit machinery.

**A3. Batch as decision unit** — one signature covers the cohort. Rejected: the decision unit is the deployment (commitment 3). Batch-level sign-off converts a presentation optimization into a guarantee breach — per-change "looked at" and "recorded sign-off" become unverifiable per change, and incident attribution returns to "which change in the batch," reintroducing exactly the queryability hole the framing commits to closing. The cohort summary is a report; the signatures are per-item. This is the direction's line between re-engineering attention and re-engineering approval, and I hold it as firmly as the automation designs hold their opposite line.

**A4. Time-boxed review slots as the primary mechanism.** Subsumed, not adopted as primary: slotting without cohorting leaves context-switching unaddressed; slotting without WIP limits just delays the undifferentiated queue. Slots are a scheduling component within cohort-based batching, not a mechanism on their own.

**A5. Learned ML tiering classifier from day one.** Rejected as the first implementation, for the no-labels reason the classify design's A1 gives — but with a different consequence: this design *generates* tier-disagreement labels continuously and nearly for free (reviewer flags), so a learned tier classifier becomes a legitimate later upgrade with a materially lower bar than in the automation direction, because its errors remain attention errors. The deterministic-first choice is about auditability and debuggability, not a permanent exclusion of learned components.

**A6. Score-based tiering.** Rejected for the same conjunctive reasons as the prior proposals: a score would present the reviewer a number to interpret instead of the failed-predicate list the brief needs, and hidden compensation across axes (clean tests buying off a wide blast radius) is wrong when the product is attention guidance keyed to *why* judgment is needed.

**A7. Briefs without tiering.** Rejected as the sole mechanism: per-change briefs improve review quality but leave the queue undifferentiated, and the routine-behind-judgment latency problem — which the framing names as the deployer-facing symptom of the same disease — is untouched. Tiering is what routing and batching key off; without it, the design is half the direction.

**A8. Reviewer self-selected tiers** — let reviewers choose items by interest or prestige. Rejected: attention allocation becomes taste, uncovered classes starve, and the classification signal degrades to noise. The tier is machine-issued advice; the human's disagreement with it is a recorded signal, not the assignment mechanism.

### Disagreements with earlier proposals

The classify-and-auto-approve and policy-replaces-approval proposals share mechanics I adopt without re-litigation — provenance classes, conjunctive predicates, deterministic evaluation, artifact binding, append-only records. The disagreements are about which problem the design solves and what it spends to solve it.

**D1. On what the problem is.** Both prior designs accept the efficiency reading's premise — the rubber-stamping disease is cured by changing the decision-maker, partially or wholly. Both then concede the same wound: the classify design's F11 admits guarantee (a) "does not survive for the auto-approved class in per-change form," and the policy design's matrix row for g1 marks it "modified" and replaces per-change human looking with sampled and reactive substitutes. My contention: the framing's success metric is attention quality ("same hours with better attention counts as a win"), and both designs spend the framing's most contested asset — the Q1 security license — to buy a throughput result the framing has explicitly subordinated, while modifying the one guarantee the sponsor could actually name in preference to the one they added because it was expressible in compiled form. That inversion is the wrong trade before Q1 is answered. My design keeps all three guarantees untouched, runs with no license, and delivers the attention-quality outcome directly. If the measured, differentiated human gate still catches nothing, my design is falsified — and that falsification *is* the evidence that licenses theirs. The correct order is this design as the common trunk and theirs as the branches that grow from the data it produces; the compaction should treat that ordering as a design property, not a conservatism.

**D2. On Q4.** Both designs engineer around the platform team's hoped-for interpretation of "no side channel" — a mode controller inside the gate for the classify design, a stage bypass for the policy design, which its own I2 calls "the sharpest possible reading." Mine never needs Q4 answered at all: there are no class-differentiated gates because there are no class-differentiated decisions. The platform dependency that remains — review-UI presentation and disposition capture — is a different kind of ask, with a working fallback if refused. A design that needs a permissive answer to a question nobody has asked yet has ordered its dependencies badly; this one does not ask.

**D3. On the shadow oracle — my sharpest methodological objection.** The classify design's F10 concedes its shadow measures "agreement with a weak oracle" — a rubber-stamping human — and the policy design adopts the concession plus a second-order weakness (encodability classification is itself a human judgment). Both then propose a zero-divergence streak as the cutover criterion. I hold that criterion is weaker than either design admits: an evaluator that has learned to agree with a rubber-stamping oracle is the pathology the framing set out to cure, and a zero-divergence streak cannot distinguish "agrees because correct" from "agrees because equally rubber." My design has no shadow problem: the human never stops deciding, so there is no second decision-maker to validate against a corrupted oracle. The adapted discipline — measured, revertible presentation rollout against an instrumented baseline — measures what it claims to measure. I record this as a genuine disagreement about the epistemics of the shadow period, not a preference.

**D4. On accountability.** Both designs attach named accountable humans to machine decisions those humans never looked at. The policy design's own F2 concedes "accountability as practiced may be nominal" — I adopt their self-criticism as my argument. Under the framing's priority order, safety is first, and a per-change human who looked and signed is not ceremony: it is the control. Accountability for a *system that* looked is a different product, and whether it satisfies security's definition of a sign-off is precisely Q1 — which is why those designs cannot run and mine can.

**D5. On spending the unsecured pool.** The policy design self-reports as "the heaviest consumer of security-team time among the readings" for a pool the framing records as unsecured. The classify design bills thresholds, sampled re-review, and override review against the same unsecured pool and the fixed reviewer capacity. Mine's standing security burden is reading reports security would need for Q1 anyway, and its reviewer-pool tax is near zero because the review is the measurement. Designs that spend capacity the framing says may not exist are licensed on an assumption; this one is licensed on the current rules.

**D6. On the endpoint question — no convergence expected.** They hold that re-engineered attention is at best a waystation en route to automating the decision. I hold it can be the endpoint for the problem the framing actually scoped: "routine-approval volume and the reviewer attention waste it produces," with success defined as attention quality. Their velocity argument (attention can't defeat fixed capacity in the limit) is a capacity argument, and capacity-bound automation is a different problem than the one scoped — one that the security conversation, not this design stage, owns. The settlement is empirical and it is the same measurement both sides accept must come first: the instrumented baseline of what today's human gate blocks. Until that exists, I will not call a design that modifies the sponsor's named guarantee safe on the strength of a license the security team has not given. That is the disagreement, and the compaction should record it, not reconcile it.

## Design proposal — differentiated-human-review

### Approach

This direction is **approval-preserving attention engineering**: the human decision stays the decision, everywhere, at every tier, for every deployment. Nothing is automated in the decision path. What is re-engineered is everything around the decision — how changes are classified, how they are queued, how they are presented, and how the fixed pool of reviewer attention is allocated. This is the joint development of the framing's control-quality and information readings: Idea 3's advisory variant alone was correctly diagnosed as inert ("decision maker unchanged — the seed's complaint persists"), and Reading 4 was endorsed as viable ("the sponsor endorsed that these can reclaim attention without renegotiation. Ceiling: may not reduce hours much — acceptable under the redefined success metric"). The advisory variant fails because it adds information without reallocating anything; information dropped into an unchanged first-come queue is wasted on the queue shape that produces rubber-stamping. Reallocation without information is blind. This direction does both, but does not cross the line into automating the decision.

Core commitments, stated so later agents can challenge them precisely:

1. **No deployment decision is automated.** There is no classifier output that authorizes a deployment, no qualification that bypasses the gate, no windowed default that converts silence into consent. The gate is the gate: a named human approves or denies, exactly as today. The framing's constraint "any automated approval must be inspectable after the fact by the security team" has no antecedent in this direction, because there is no automated approval. This is not a conservative stance taken to hedge; it is the direction's premise.

2. **The engineering target is attention allocation, not the decision.** The framing's decision 16 records the diagnosis this direction builds on: "the sponsor suspects uniform application of one approval rule is the root cause (tiering, routing, and batching could reclaim attention without touching the approval concept)." Today the pipeline allocates fixed reviewer capacity first-come-first-served across every change identically, one full review screen per change regardless of what it touches. That allocation is the object of the redesign: **tiering** (every change is classified into an attention tier at pipeline time), **routing** (changes surface to the right reviewers by tier and domain), **batching** (routine changes are reviewed in deliberate, pre-digested sessions, not as a drip of interruptions).

3. **Classification output is evidence, never a decision.** The tier, the rule trace, and the prepared brief are *presentation artifacts* attached to the reviewer's screen — the same epistemic status as test results. They never determine, gate, or veto a human decision. This is the semantic line the other directions cross and this one refuses to: a misclassification here cannot admit a bad deployment; it can only misallocate scrutiny depth, and scrutiny depth is made visible, measured, and revisable rather than assumed uniform as it is today. The framing's own language distinguishes the readings by exactly this; this direction is Reading 4 taken to its full conclusion, plus the control-quality goal the sponsorship actually wants (decision 2: "attention quality, not hours").

4. **The guarantees survive unmodified.** All three, in their original per-change form. (a) "a human has looked at every change" — literally true for every deployment, including the largest routine class. (b) "a recorded named sign-off" — every deployment carries a human approval act with a name, as today. (c) "a named accountable party for every deployment, including automated ones" — satisfiable with no machinery at all because there are no automated deployments; the approver named in (b) is the accountable party, and the deployer remains the recorded initiating actor. The sponsor's D14 default ("the policy evaluation counts as the decision") is moot here: there is a decision-act on every deployment, so the unauditable-non-decision problem the other directions must engineer around never arises.

5. **The direction's ceiling is stated by its proposer, not discovered by its critics.** This direction does not remove the approval queue from the deployer's path. A routine deployment still waits on a human. What it delivers on the latency axis: predictable wait for routine changes (batch cadence gives a review-slot commitment instead of an opaque queue), fewer rejection-rework cycles (enqueue screening surfaces mechanically checkable problems before a reviewer spends time on them), and faster per-item review (briefs replace raw-diff recon). What it delivers on the metric the framing actually chose — attention quality — is the full instrument: the same hours, applied where they matter, with the waste made visible. The framing accepted this trade when it endorsed Reading 4 ("may not reduce hours much — acceptable under the redefined success metric"); this design takes that decision at its word and does not engineer around it.

6. **Value is not gated on the security conversation.** Framing question 1 — whether security accepts retrospectively inspectable *automated decisions* — licenses or kills both prior directions' live modes in their own words. This direction has no live mode to license: there is no cutover from anything to anything. The tiering/routing/batching layer is a presentation and allocation change to an unchanged gate, deliverable in full before, during, or entirely without the Q1 answer. For a framing whose stated confidence (0.7) is held down chiefly by exactly that unresolved question, a direction whose delivered value does not depend on its answer is not the cautious choice; it is the only one that can be shipped without betting on an outcome nobody controls.

7. **The new failure class is misallocation, not misdecision.** The worst thing this direction can do is route a change that deserved deep scrutiny into shallow batch review, or waste deep attention on changes that needed little. Both directions of error are *visible humans acting on recorded evidence*, instrumented by escalation tracking and disposition capture, and correctable by rule revision. The direction cannot fail open; it cannot fail closed differently than today; it can only fail to reallocate well, which is measurable and tunable.

### System primitives

**Evidence object.** Adopted from the earlier proposals with the same provenance discipline — `{id, kind, value, source-class, origin, capture-time, integrity, derived-from[]}`, `source-class ∈ {author-supplied, pipeline-derived, pipeline-observed}`, bound by hash to the deployment artifact. Provenance classes remain load-bearing here for a different reason than the automation designs gave: the brief is now what a human reads, so a laundered author claim no longer risks an automated decision — it risks misdirecting the one resource the whole direction conserves, which is attention. The discipline is identical; the consumer is human.

**The attention tier.** A deterministic, conjunctive classification computed at pipeline time, assigning every change to exactly one of three tiers. The predicate content is largely the shared machinery of the prior designs — composition bounding, blast-radius bounding, failure-detectability, no-special-infra, history-clean, identity-attested, artifact-integrity — but the consumer is the queue, not the decision: **T1 (batch-screened)** — every mechanical predicate holds affirmatively on independently generated or pipeline-observed evidence; **T3 (deep review)** — special infrastructure (feature flags, traffic routing, credentials, migrations, security settings), the classifier's own control surface, wide blast radius, or open incidents on affected services; **T2 (individual judgment)** — everything else, the default. The error analysis is asymmetric in a way the automation designs could not afford: over-inclusion in T1 misroutes scrutiny depth (a human still decides, with an escalation affordance); over-inclusion in T3 wastes attention. Only the first direction of error is safety-relevant, so T1 is deliberately conservative and expands only on measured escalation quiet (below). Untiered is defined as not-T1: degradation conserves attention rather than spending it badly.

**The attention policy.** The versioned, owned document consisting of the tiering rules, the depth rubric (the tier-by-tier contract for how deep review should go), and the routing matrix (which pools own which domains at which tiers). This is deliberately and loudly *not* an approval policy: it governs allocation of a fixed human resource, not admission to production. A wrong clause costs attention; a permissive clause risk is bounded by the fact that a human always decides. It is versioned and published because audit must be able to say which allocation rules were in force when a change was presented — but its governance is lighter than the automation designs' policy governance (see Operational complexity), and that lightness is itself a designed, named surface attacked by failure mode F5.

**The review brief.** The pre-digested reviewer screen, generated deterministically from the frozen snapshot: per-rule outcomes with source-class visible on every input, a structured diff summary (never raw diff scrolling), blast-radius summary from the pipeline-maintained graph, monitoring coverage summary for the change's failure-mode family, test summary, the tier and its trace — and the **policy assistance** the direction statement names: the written review rubric for this class (the depth contract, the checklist), plus the history of similar changes in this service/class (recent dispositions, common denial reasons, rework rate). The brief is versioned and content-addressed; disposition records store its fingerprint and generator version so the audit question "what was the reviewer actually shown" is replayable. The brief is also shown to the deployer at enqueue — not as a second gate but as the same presentation the reviewer will see, which aligns deployer incentives toward the scrutiny the change will receive rather than away from it.

**The batch abstract.** Metadata for T1 batch sessions: the grouping key (domain, class, or both), member count, the commonality summary (what is identical across the batch — a reviewer should read this once), and the **outlier marks** (the items in the batch that differ from the group in any safety-relevant dimension, which the batch construction flags for full opening). A batch is a presentation vehicle. It is never a decision unit; the abstract records per-item sign-off status so a batch cannot close with an unsigned item.

**Disposition record.** The superset of today's approval log: `{decision-id, disposition ∈ {approved, denied, reworked, escalated}, approver, timestamp, tier-at-presentation, brief-fingerprint, escalation?, annotations[]}` where annotations let the reviewer anchor a denial or rework to specific brief rules ("denied on blast-radius, see rule 3") instead of free text. This is the framing's baseline instrumentation (decision 5: denials, reworks, and incident attribution must become queryable) and it is this direction's phase one deliverable, not a detour from it.

**Escalation record.** A reviewer-initiated re-tier with a structured reason. This is the direction's divergence signal: an escalation is a human disagreeing with where attention was routed. Escalation patterns feed attention-policy revision exactly as shadow divergences feed policy revision in the automation designs — but the stakes are attention, not admissions, so the revision loop is lighter and faster.

**Attention ledger.** The standing measurement of the thing the framing redefined success around: time-to-review by tier, queue depth and staleness, batch cadence planned vs. achieved, per-item sign-off times, rework and denial rates by class, escalation rates by rule, incident attribution by tier. Because "attention quality" (decision 2) has no naive operationalization, the design proposes proxies and names them as proxies: (i) anchored denials (denials that cite specific brief rules — measurably more informative review than free text), (ii) escalation confirmations (did escalated items, on re-review, produce denials/rework — i.e., did deeper scrutiny find what the batch would have missed), (iii) time-per-item by tier against the rubric's expectation, (iv) incident attribution by tier. Proxies, not the thing itself; the thing itself is reviewed by humans in the escalation loop.

### Architecture

Four components, one trust domain, no actuators.

**Evidence preparation** (this team) — adopted from the shared machinery: snapshot assembly, provenance conversion, the blast-radius graph consumer. **Brief and tier generator** (this team) — a deterministic, versioned function `(snapshot, attention-policy-version, generator-digest) → {tier, trace, brief, batch-abstract}`. Determinism requirements are adopted from the earlier proposals (no wall clock, no evaluation-time I/O, content-addressed generator, replay-regression over the disposition corpus gating every generator release) because the brief is now the artifact a human decides on, and "what was the reviewer shown" must be reconstructible. Strictly cheaper than the automation designs' evaluator: the function prepares a view; it does not decide anything, so a generator bug can mislead attention but cannot admit a deployment — the failure's blast radius is a wasted review, and the regression gate sizes to that.

**Queue and triage presentation** (platform team's approval UI) — the tier display, routing defaults, batch session construction, escalation capture, SLA visibility, and disposition capture. This is the platform team's surface, and the entire platform dependency of the direction. It is a view and a recorder. There is no mode controller, no bypass, no enforcement engine, no live/shadow mode pair. The strongest architectural statement of this direction is an absence: **nothing in the pipeline responds to the tier with changed control flow.** The gate executes exactly as today, byte-for-byte, in every phase.

**Disposition store** (append-only, shared with the baseline instrumentation) and **measurement loop** (this team) — the attention ledger, escalation divergence reports, SLA dashboards, and the standing proxy metrics.

**Integrity.** The brief binds to the artifact hash and the attestation is shown to the reviewer: the screen asserts "this brief describes the artifact that built from this commit," so the evaluate-clean-view-dirty attack is killed at the presentation layer rather than the enforcement layer. If the binding fails, the reviewer gets the raw view and a notice — review proceeds, undegraded, on the honest materials.

**Degradation is the steady state, not a fallback.** Tier generator down → untiered queue, no batches, reviewers see the raw screen: exactly today. Brief generator buggy → briefs suppressed with a notice, raw view shown: exactly today. Attention policy version lapses → conservative defaults (T2 routing, no batches): exactly today. The direction's failure surface is literally a return to the status quo, because the status quo is not something the direction replaces — it is the substrate the direction decorates.

**Phasing.** P0 — disposition capture + attention-ledger bootstrapping: the platform team's smallest ask (a data write and a field on the existing approval screen), which doubles as the baseline measurement the framing has already committed to (D5). In parallel, rubric elicitation from the approvers begins (see Implementation constraints — soft-gated, not blocking). P1 — recorded tiers: the generator runs, and reviewers see the tier, trace, and brief alongside today's unchanged screen; no routing or batching yet. Escalations and dispositions accumulate; this is the direction's tuning loop, and it is deliberately **not called a shadow period**: there is no cutover anywhere downstream, no parity gate to pass, no oracle being compared against for authorization purposes. The loop tunes routing quality. P2 — routing and batching presentation, per domain, graduated: batch sessions for T1, domain-default routing for T2/T3, SLA dashboards live before any commitment is made. The per-domain graduation gate is escalation-rate asymmetry (escalation rate into review below a queue-ops-set threshold over a minimum volume per class), set by queue operations, not security — an attention-quality gate, not a safety gate. P3 — standing loops: attention-policy revision cadence, brief feedback review, measurement audits.

### User workflows

**Deployer.**

1. Push → pipeline runs → evidence prep and tier generation happen with the other gates. Before the approval request is submitted, the deployer sees the **enqueue screening**: the tier, the mechanical check summary, and the brief. If the mechanical conjunction fails (e.g., the change touches traffic routing with no monitoring coverage; the change touches a service with an open incident), the deployer sees it *now*, with the same anchored presentation the reviewer will see — not after a reviewer has spent twenty minutes reconstructing and denying it. The screening is non-binding: there is no block, no override, no new path; the deployer may enqueue anyway and the flags ride along in the brief.
2. On enqueue, routing follows the tier. **T1**: the change joins the next batch session for its domain, and the deployer sees a review-slot commitment — "your change will be reviewed in the next routine batch session, expected within N hours" — where N is an SLA set from measured batch cadence (below), not from aspiration. **T2**: the change joins the visible pool queue, where any reviewer can claim it; the UI shows queue position and staleness. **T3**: the change routes to the named accountable reviewer pool for the affected domain, with an interrupt-appropriate alert — T3 arrivals are events, not queue items.
3. During review the deployer sees progress, not a boolean: which reviewer claimed the item, how far the review has proceeded through the brief, and — for the anchored-denial case — the denial arrives stamped with the specific rules and evidence that failed. That transforms the rework loop: the resubmission references the prior disposition, and the second review opens with a diff-against-prior-brief rather than a cold screen.
4. Nothing in any deplorer-facing screen ever says "auto," "qualified," "admitted," or "policy-evaluated." The vocabulary is the unchanged vocabulary, because the decision is the unchanged decision. No terminology-migration program exists in this direction; that program (which the policy-replaces design correctly identifies as "a program, not a rename") is a cost the other directions pay for having changed the concept. This direction does not change the concept, so it does not pay the cost.

**Reviewer.**

Three review modes replace one undifferentiated mode:

| Tier | Presentation | Evidence depth | Norm | Sign-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Batch session, pre-scheduled, calendar-reserved, 8–12 items per ≤30-minute session | Batch commonality summary once; per-item brief; outlier-marked items opened in full | Shallow-but-present screening on strong prepared evidence; every item individually confirmed | Per-item named sign-off, one confirmation per item on the pre-loaded brief — never a batch-level sign-off |
| T2 | Visible pool queue, oldest-first default, domain defaults | Full brief; raw drill-down one click away | Individual judgment, as today but evidence-prepped | Named sign-off, recorded as always |
| T3 | Routed to named domain reviewers, scheduled deep-review blocks | Full brief plus full raw evidence; no summarization shortcuts | Unhurried deep review; the framing has already accepted this cohort may get slower | Named sign-off |

The per-item sign-off rule in the T1 row is structural, not advisory: batching the presentation must never batch the sign-off, because guarantees (b) and (c) are per-deployment. The UI enforces this by shape — a batch cannot close with an unsigned item, and each item requires its own confirm click on its own brief. This is not automation; it is cheapening per-item ceremony to match the depth the item earned, which is exactly attention engineering.

Two affordances matter beyond the table. **Escalation**: one click per item bumps the tier with a structured reason (free text allowed), and escalated items land with the escalation note attached. Escalation is a first-class, tracked act — it is the mechanism by which routing errors become visible, and its reason corpus is the input to attention-policy revision. **Anchored dispositions**: denials and reworks can cite brief rules ("denied on `history-clean`: open incident INC-4471 on `payments`"), which makes the reviewer's reasoning queryable for the first time — this is the same disposition-capture instrumentation the framing committed to as baseline measurement, but in this direction it is the reviewer's tool, not merely an audit obligation.

**Approver responsibility lands where it always did.** The named approver on T3 — and on any other tier — decides and is recorded, as today. This direction adds no new accountable role to the decision path: the framings' guarantee (c) is satisfied by the same named human that guarantee (b) records, because the decision-act still exists for every deployment. No policy owner is drafted into per-deployment accountability.

**Reviewer-pool lead (queue operations).** A new operationally real role: owns the routing map defaults, the batch cadence against measured reviewer availability, the SLA commitments, escalation review, and the monthly tiering-retro where escalation patterns and disposition outcomes decide whether T1 expands or contracts. The role is real work drawn from existing reviewer seniority — it formalizes queue coordination that today happens implicitly, and the direction says so rather than pretending the coordination is free.

**Attention policy owner.** Owns the tiering rules, the depth rubric, and their version history. Lighter than the automation designs' policy-owner role in exactly the ways that matter: this owner is accountable for allocation quality (measured by escalation divergence), not per-deployment accountable on every change that transits under their rules. The governance asymmetry is intentional and is attacked by failure mode F5.

**Security.** Consumes the measurement reports, optionally reviews attention-policy diffs, and optionally holds a veto over attention-policy changes (the design accommodates it as a standing review option). Crucially, security time is consumed **nowhere on the critical path**: no threshold must be set, no capacity must be secured, no inspection license must be granted before any phase ships. The direction's standing draw on the security pool rounds to zero in phases 0–2, which matters in an org where the framing records security capacity as unsecured and the pool as fixed.

**Incident responder.** Implicated deployments resolve by decision-id, and the disposition record carries the tier, brief fingerprint, and annotations. Incident review can now ask — and the record can now answer — the two questions the rubber-stamping regime could not even formulate: *was this change presented accurately* (replay the brief from the fingerprint) and *was it presented at the right depth* (tier vs. what the change turned out to be). Neither prior direction's records answer the second question about their residual human gate; this direction answers it about every deployment.

### Failure modes

**F1. Mis-tiering into T1 — a consequential change batch-screened shallow.** The direction's central risk, stated without euphemism: it is not a false negative on a decision (a human decided; the gate held), it is a false routing of scrutiny depth — it is the one place this direction can make the safety picture *worse than status quo in kind*, because today's uniform review is uniformly whatever-depth, while T1 review is deliberately shallow. Mitigations: T1 admission is a conjunction of independently evidenced predicates, not a score (adopted from the prior design for the same reason — a score buys off scrutiny depth with cleanliness, and this criterion is conjunctive); batch construction marks outliers and the session norm requires them opened in full; escalation is one click and its rate is the standing T1-expansion gate; conservative start (T1 begins as the mechanically provable core — content, docs, attested pinned-image bumps — and expands only on measured escalation quiet); incident attribution by tier queries how often batch-screened changes are implicated. Residual: scrutiny depth is a property of human behavior, and this direction's honest limit is that it makes depth allocation *visible, instrumented, and revisable* instead of uniformly unexamined. It cannot machine-enforce depth. That residual is also the status quo's residual, unmeasured today and measured under this direction.

**F2. Batch blur.** Twelve similar items produce complacency; the batch reads as one item and the one that differed gets the group's coat of paint. Mitigations: bounded session sizes, homogeneous grouping with outlier marking (the batch construction is deliberately built around surfacing the odd item, not burying it), per-item sign-off as a structural UI fact, and batch abstracts that record per-item confirmations so an unsigned member is visibly impossible. Residual: a reviewer who confirms without looking is undetectable by any machinery — which is true of every human gate ever fielded, and is the framing's own rubber-stamping premise, here bounded rather than eliminated.

**F3. Queue starvation and cherry-picking.** T2's open pool invites skimming easy items while hard ones age; a routed change whose default reviewer is away silently stalls. Mitigations: staleness bins visible to pool leads with escalation thresholds; domain routing gives each change a *default* owner so "nobody is looking" has a name; oldest-first presentation defaults. All defaults, none enforceable — the failure this cannot fully kill is a social one, and the design says so rather than building permission machinery that would import the shadow-gate failure below.

**F4. Routing hardens into a shadow gate.** Presentation defaults — which queues a reviewer is shown, which pool "owns" a domain — accrete into de facto permissions; a change stuck in an unowned-looking T2 quadrant effectively waits on a gate nobody signed off on. This is the direction's own version of the side-channel constraint. Mitigation: the invariant that routing is default-presentation only, never filtering, is written into the attention policy and checked in platform UI review ("can any reviewer see and claim any item? if not, this is a gate and needs the gate's governance"). The direction's entire framing depends on this invariant holding.

**F5. Attention-policy erosion — the dial problem relocated.** The prior classify design warned against thresholds that "feel operational rather than governance-grade." This direction's tiering rules are deliberately lighter-governed — and therefore the drift risk is real: a slowly loosened T1 ("we know this class is fine"), a quietly broadened batch session, and scrutiny depth erodes without any decision ever having been automated. The direction's counter is not heavy governance but heavy visibility: every attention-policy change publishes its *predicted* routing distribution impact before release and is checked against *measured* escalation and disposition shifts after; a T1 expansion that produces an escalation spike is reverted by policy revision, and the reversion is as cheap as the change. Light governance with loud telemetry is the designed trade; F5 is the attack on it, and the design names the attack.

**F6. Brief rot.** The brief drifts from reality — a stale blast-radius graph, a misleading diff summary, a monitoring-coverage claim that predates a purge. A bad decision under a bad brief is still a human decision; the damage is to the direction's entire premise, which is that prepared evidence improves attention. Mitigations: deterministic generation with replay-regression gating generator releases over the disposition corpus (adopted, cheaper than the prior designs' version since the brief decides nothing); provenance display on every brief input so a launderable claim is visibly a claim; and a brief-feedback channel where reviewers — the brief's consumers, and its best critics — file "this brief was misleading" reports that feed the revision loop.

**F7. The enqueue flag becomes a de facto gate.** The non-binding screening prediction accrues social force: "red-flagged changes don't get enqueued" becomes team norm, and the direction has acquired an undocumented gate with no owner and no audit record — precisely the shadow-control the org's constraints exist to prevent. Mitigation: the flag's non-binding status is not a UI footnote but a measured quantity — the rate at which flagged changes are enqueued anyway, and the denial rate among them, are ledger metrics; a collapse in flagged-but-enqueued rate is an alert that the social gate has formed. This is a communication and ownership problem and is treated as one.

**F8. SLA theater.** A review-slot commitment ("next batch, within N hours") that the measured cadence cannot actually deliver turns predictability into broken promises, which are worse than no promises. The design's rule: the SLA is set *from* measured cadence, never before it; the dashboard ships before the commitment does; and a missed SLA is an operational incident in the attention ledger, not a dashboard footnote.

**F9. The measurement loop lies.** Adopted from the earlier proposals wholesale: an unmeasured measurement system quietly invalidates the escalation-divergence gate that tunes tiering. Lighter here — nothing downstream is a cutover decision on it — but the escalation-rate threshold that gates T1 expansion inherits the same obligation to be audited.

**F10. Adoption failure — reviewers do not batch.** The machinery delivers surfaces; the reallocation of attention is a human act. If reviewers decline batch sessions, keep grabbing ad hoc, and treat the brief as decoration, the direction collapses to exactly the advisory-ui variant the framing correctly dismissed as inert. This is the direction's only true dependency, and it is a social one: the fixed pool must adopt the new allocation or the design degrades to A1 with extra screens. The design's mitigations are calendar-reserved sessions, visible outcomes (escalation stats, anchored dispositions showing the brief working), and the framing's own success metric, which makes adoption the *goal* rather than a nice-to-have — but the dependency is named because naming it is the only honest treatment.

### Operational complexity

The headline is a structural fact: **this is the cheapest of the three directions to operate.** One decision verb, one actor class, one accountability shape, no live/shadow mode pair to operate, no override machinery, no per-deployment policy-owner accountability, no objections channel, no certificate semantics, no terminology migration program, and degradation that is literally the status quo rather than a designed fallback. The policy-replaces design conceded "two decision semantics on one pipeline" as a real cost (its D4); this direction also avoids the classify design's softer version of the same cost — dual actors (machine and human) for one verb, which still propagates a class distinction through runbooks, incident tooling, and audit training. The near-zero standing complexity is a designed property, and the design claims it against both predecessors.

The real standing costs, named with their owners:

**The platform-UI work is the direction's entire enforcement-adjacent surface, and it is incremental.** P0 asks for a disposition-capture field on the existing approval screen (the baseline measurement the framing has already committed to — not a cost this direction uniquely imposes). P1 asks to display the tier and brief on that screen. P2 asks for batch construction, routing defaults, and staleness surfaces — the largest ask, a queue-presentation rework, but still a view change on the unchanged gate. No phase changes control flow; the platform team is never asked to arbitrate the "no side channel" interpretation for differentiated internal gates (framing Q4), because there is no differentiated gate — only differentiated queues. Q4 is inert in its strong form here. What remains live is Q11 — the platform team's change process gates changes to the approval surface — with the smallest possible ask of the three directions.

**Queue operations is new staffing.** The reviewer-pool-lead role formalizes coordination that today happens implicitly (or does not happen at all, which is part of the rubber-stamping story). It is real work: routing-map upkeep, cadence calibration against measured availability, escalation review, the monthly tiering retro. The direction states the staffing need rather than absorbing it silently into the fixed pool.

**The batch calendar is the human-adoption cost the automation directions do not have.** Batch sessions consume reviewer calendar time in advance, in deliberate blocks. This changes working patterns — the fixed-capacity pool must commit hours to scheduled T1 sessions and T3 deep-review blocks instead of grabbing reviews ad hoc. It is a change-management cost, and it is the point: the attention reallocation does not happen in software. The design budgets it as a rollout activity, not an assumption.

**The measurement loop is standing ops, sized below the automation designs'.** The attention ledger, escalation divergence, SLA dashboards, and the measurement audit run continuously, but feed revision loops rather than a cutover gate. Replay burden is audit-demand-driven (replay a disposition's brief from its fingerprint), plus the digest-generator regression over the disposition corpus on release — the same mechanics as the prior designs, at lower stakes and lower frequency.

**The latency honesty, as an operational property.** The batch cadence imposes a floor on T1 wait: a change arriving just after a session waits for the next one. Batching trades mean wait for predictability and attention quality — the batch counteracts the delay at the per-item service-time level (warm context, pre-loaded briefs, fewer rework re-entries from enqueue screening) and the net effect on mean queue delay is *measurable, not predictable ex ante*. It is plausible this direction mildly increases mean wait for the routine class. Under decision 2 — attention quality, hours saved "a proxy, not the goal" — that trade is acceptable as written; the design records rather than hides that this is the one axis where it may be net-negative against the seed's original complaint, which was deployer delay. If the sponsor privately retains wait-time as the true objective, this direction underdelivers and the automation directions overdeliver; the framing's own decisions say attention quality is the objective, so this direction complies and states the dependence.

**Degradation runs monotonically to the status quo.** Engine down, brief buggy, policy lapsed — each resolves to "untiered queue, raw screen, same gate," which is today. The runbook has one line. This is operational simplicity of exactly the kind the framing's reliability priority (2) asks for.

### Security implications

**The automation constraint is inert.** "Any automated approval must be inspectable after the fact by the security team" — there is no automated approval, so there is nothing to license, nothing to inspect under that rule, and no Q1 dependency anywhere in the direction's delivery path. The prior designs both declare their live modes conditional on the security conversation's answer; this direction's entire value is unconditional on it. For a framing whose confidence is dominated by exactly that unresolved question, this is the direction's security posture in one sentence.

**The security surface is the allocation, not the decision.** The tiering rules are where the safety boundary passes through this direction — a mis-tier routes scrutiny depth, and scrutiny depth is a screening-quality input. So the security-relevant artifacts are: the attention policy (published, diff-visible, veto available to security on request), the T1 admission predicate set (conservative by construction), the escalation divergence reports, and — the one genuinely new security capability this direction adds — **incident attribution by tier**, which lets security ask whether the batch-screened class is overrepresented in incidents and demand a T1 contraction if it ever is. The direction invites exactly this scrutiny: its safety case gets *stronger* the more security looks at the allocation data, because the direction's claim is that attention reallocation improves screening quality — a falsifiable claim with the disposition and attribution data now available to falsify it.

**The whole safety increment rides on the framing's assumption A5** — "attention quality serves screening quality," which the framing itself lists as "plausible and consistent with the answers, but not yet evidenced." This direction has no machine-checkable safety boundary of its own; it is pure attention engineering on top of the unchanged human gate. If A5 is false — if rubber-stamping is actually adequate screening and attention quality is a luxury metric — this direction's security upside evaporates and it retains only "no worse than unchanged," while the automation directions retain machine-checkable first-term safety of their own. The design records this exposure against itself as the strongest honest criticism it faces, and notes the cheap, real test: the P0 disposition data plus the attention ledger can measure whether shallow-screening patterns correlate with missed denials *before any queue change ships*. If the test fails, this direction should be re-evaluated on the merits, and the prior designs' automation bets deserve their weight.

**Brief gameability is fought at evidence provenance, as in the prior designs** — author-supplied claims cannot satisfy tiering predicates; the tier and brief derive from mechanical computation and pipeline observation — but at lower stakes: a shaped change that reads well in a brief still faces a human who can drill down, escalate, and deny, and whose denial is anchored and queryable. Additionally, the deployer sees the same brief at enqueue, so the obvious gaming move — optimizing the change toward the presentation — optimizes it toward the scrutiny, which is incentive alignment rather than concealment.

**The enqueue-screening flag is the one surface with social-gate potential** (F7), and its measurement — flagged-but-enqueued rates and their outcomes — is the standing security-relevant watch on it.

### Implementation constraints

**Platform-team dependency, stacked smallest-to-largest, each phase valuable alone.** The direction never asks the platform team for a mode controller, a bypass, or any control-flow change — its ask is a data-capture field (P0), a screen augmentation (P1), and a queue-presentation rework (P2). Q11 (the platform team's change process) still applies to all of it; the direction's position is that the *weight* of the ask is a separate variable from its existence, and this is the lightest ask of the three directions by a class.

**Inherited open questions, sorted into live and inert.** Inert for this direction: Q1 (no automated decision to license), Q4 in its strong form (no differentiated gate), Q5 (no policy-only deployment ever exists), Q6 (no false-negative decision threshold exists; the escalation-rate threshold that replaces it is a queue-ops attention-quality parameter, not a security parameter). Live, in weakened form: Q2 (the T1 criteria values remain criteria-work — but misallocation, not authorization, is what a wrong value buys, so the values can start conservative-and-small and expand on escalation evidence rather than blocking cutover), Q3 (answered by the direction: machine prepares, human decides — stated as the direction's choice, not smuggled), Q8 (answered: deployers see the tier and brief pre-enqueue; same rationale as the prior designs — the boundary is learnable by probing anyway, so gameability is fought at evidence provenance, and deployer visibility is what makes enqueue screening work), Q9 (relocated: an attention-policy owner is required for P2, but the role is lighter-gauge than the automation designs' policy owner and is not a live-mode gate), Q11 (live, weakest form). Inherited unchanged and not load-bearing: Q7 (the emergency-revert gap — this direction adds no override surface and changes nothing about the revert path; the gap remains the org's, as it was before this direction existed). Non-blocking: Q10 (guarantee completeness — this direction does not compile guarantees into rules, so it does not need the list to be complete; the rubric elicitation it runs in parallel with P0 advances Q10 as a side effect by making the approvers articulate their standards, in a context where an incomplete answer wastes attention rather than falsifying certificates).

**Determinism is a build-time property of the brief generator**, for exactly the replay-grade audit reason the prior designs demand of their evaluators — "what the reviewer was shown" must be reconstructible — but sized to a view: no policy language, no totality proof over a decision function, just a versioned, content-addressed generator with a regression over the disposition corpus.

**Queueing honesty is a constraint.** SLAs are set from measured batch cadence, which is set from measured reviewer availability. The direction forbids promising a review-slot commitment before the dashboard exists, on the failure-mode F8 grounds that broken predictability is worse than none.

**Staffing minimally required:** P0–P1 can run with this team alone plus the platform platform's field. P2 requires the attention-policy owner and a named queue-ops lead from the reviewer pool. Security staffing required for delivery: none. The direction's delivery path is the only one of the three that a fixed, already-loaded staffing picture can absorb without waiting on a pool the framing records as unsecured.

### Alternatives considered

**A1. Advisory-only recommendation — the tier and brief shown in the review screen, nothing else.** This is Idea 3's advisory-UI variant, which the framing itself noted leaves "the seed's complaint" in place. Rejected as a terminal design for exactly that reason: information without reallocation is delivered into a queue shape that manufactures rubber-stamping, so the information lands on the same wasted attention it was meant to fix. The advisory content is not rejected — it is the brief, adopted wholesale — but it is *necessary and insufficient*; the direction's work is the allocation layer around it, and without the allocation layer the direction degrades to the inert variant the framing already discounted. This is also the direction's stated fallback state if adoption fails (F10), which is why the fallback is a failure mode and not the design.

**A2. Default-approve-with-a-window** — the other half of Idea 3. Rejected, harder. The framing's own words apply: "a de facto automation and must then meet the automation bar of ideas 1/2." Every dependency this direction exists to avoid — the security license (Q1), the baseline-parity proof before cutover, the false-negative semantics, the override machinery — is imported intact, while the audit semantics are *worse* than either honest extreme: a window that closes without a human act is an approval without a name, which violates guarantee (b) and the sponsor's accountability guarantee in their most observable form. It buys nothing over honest automation and pays both prices. It is the worst point in the solution space and this direction's rejection of it is total.

**A3. Batch-level sign-off** — one name covering the batch. Rejected: violates guarantee (b) per deployment, and converts the batch from a presentation vehicle into a decision unit, which is exactly the conflation the per-item sign-off rule exists to prevent. The UI cheapens per-item confirmation (one click per pre-loaded brief); it never merges the confirmations.

**A4. Enforced routing — routed permissions rather than routed defaults.** Rejected: enforcement is where presentation becomes a gate, importing the shadow-gate failure (F4) by construction and the "no side channel" interpretation fight (Q4) that the direction exists to avoid. Routing is default-presentation by invariant; the invariant is policed at platform-UI review, and the direction accepts that this caps routing's power in exchange for keeping it out of the control plane.

**A5. Mechanical enqueue blockers** — fail-fast gates that *prevent* enqueueing a mechanistically doomed change. Rejected in favor of non-binding enqueue screening: a blocker is a new control with its own override problem and its own failure mode (wrongly blocking a deployer, who then needs a human override path — new machinery, new audit surface). The non-binding prediction has zero new failure modes, imports zero override machinery, and still kills the rework loop, because a deployer who can see the anchored mechanical failure the reviewer will see does not enqueue-and-wait. If the measured rework rate does not drop after screening ships (F7's data point), the prediction is doing nothing and should be revised — the measurement decides.

**A6. Instrument-triggered escalation** — automatic re-tiering when instrumentation detects something (anomalous cadence, matched incident pattern). Rejected: machine-initiated re-routing is automation of the routing layer, which erodes the exact property — human-mediated attention allocation — that keeps this direction off the automation license's critical path. The instrumentation may attach a *recommendation flag* to the item; a human escalates. The one-click escalation is deliberately cheap enough that the automation adds nothing.

**A7. Learned tiering.** Rejected on the same grounds as the prior designs rejected learned classification (no labels exist; determinism needed for brief replay), and additionally on grounds of overkill: tiering is a routing heuristic whose error cost is wasted attention, and a deterministic conjunctive ruleset is both sufficient and *revisable by humans who can see why a route happened* — the escalation loop depends on every tiering decision being explainable to the reviewer who disputes it.

**A8. Skill-based reviewer routing** — modeling reviewer expertise per domain and matching changes to skills. Rejected as over-machinery: domain-default routing gives each change a named default owner, which is the whole of the skill-routing value, at a fraction of the model's cost; residual expertise matching is a social problem the queue can *display* (who has reviewed this domain before, what their outcomes were) better than a matcher can solve. This direction's rule is that machinery allocates *slots*, not *people*.

### Disagreements with earlier proposals

Two preceding directions share a premise this direction rejects, and the disagreement is recorded at the level of premises, not details.

**D1. On whether the problem is the decision.** Both prior designs analyze the problem as a decision problem — who or what decides — and accept, as the price of their solutions, a modification of guarantee (a) ("a human has looked at every change"). The classify design labels its modification "the concession the reading makes" (§6); the policy design compiles g1 into four forms and marks it `modified` in its matrix. I hold this price is being paid unnecessarily: the framing's own suspicion (decision 16 — "uniform application of one approval rule is the root cause") and its own endorsed reading (Reading 4) describe a solution that pays nothing on the guarantee ledger. The prior designs treat modification of a named guarantee as an acceptable cost of an automation bet that has not been licensed; this direction treats the unlicensed bet itself as the avoidable cost. Preserving all three guarantees unmodified is not a matter of taste — it is the difference between a design that needs the framing's largest unresolved question (Q1) answered favorably and a design that needs it answered not at all. Both prior directions declare their live modes conditional on exactly that answer. A project framed at confidence 0.7 with the shortfall concentrated in that one unknown should prefer the direction whose value is unconditional on it — not as caution, but as a dependency argument: the conditional directions are options with an expiry date nobody can read; this direction delivers without the option.

**D2. On what the shadow period is for.** Both prior designs run a shadow loop that calibrates a *soon-to-be decision-maker* against an oracle they themselves concede is weak (the classify design's F10: shadow "parity" is agreement with a rubber-stamping human, and it "cannot, from shadow alone, demonstrate the absolute FNR"; the policy design adopts this and adds an encodability judgment that is itself human). Their own remedy is a cutover criterion "deliberately demonstrating too much to pass" — a heavy epistemic apparatus aimed at a risk (the rare dangerous class) that, by their own admission, the oracle cannot validate in either direction. This direction's tuning loop calibrates attention routing, where a wrong rule wastes review time and an escalation corrects it — an error cost lower by exactly the stakes that divide routing from authorizing. I hold the epistemic burden should be sized to the stakes: the prior designs spend a shadow period pretending their weak oracle can certify a decision-maker, while this direction's lighter loop is exactly as strong as its stakes require. The same instrumentation money buys calibration where calibration is possible, instead of parity theater where it is not.

**D3. On the policy owner and accountability.** The classify design, following D14, makes the policy owner an accountable party on every auto-approved deployment. This direction holds that accountability should sit with deciders, and notes the problem the prior design is solving — accountability for a decision nobody actuated — exists only inside its own premise. In this direction every deployment has a decision-act, so the approver is the accountable party and the policy owner's accountability is bounded to allocation quality, which is measurable and which they can actually discharge. The policy-replaces design's own F2 concedes the vulnerability of no-decision accountability ("accountability as practiced may be nominal"); this direction does not need to concede it because it never creates the condition.

**D4. Operational simplicity, pressed further than the prior concession.** The policy design concedes to the classify design that two decision semantics is a real operational loss (its D4). I extend that concession: the classify design itself carries a dual-semantics pipeline — machine-approved vs. human-approved, two actors for one verb — which propagates a class distinction through incident review, audit training, and runbooks. This direction is the only one of the three with one verb, one actor class, one accountability shape, in both record and UX, through every phase including delivery. On the operational-complexity dimension I was assigned to own, this is a structural ranking, not a preference, and no convergence pressure should erase it in the compaction.

**D5. On the self-application paradox.** The classify design generalizes its workaround to the framing's rollout paradox (D7) into a never-auto domain (its S5) — the machine can never evaluate its own release. This direction notes the paradox has no antecedent here: the approval mechanism is untouched, so there is no new gate to pass under the old rules, and no never-auto domain to maintain. D7's operational purchase is zero in this direction, not because the constraint is dodged but because its condition never obtains. The direction's analogue — tiers must always be present, untiered degrades to conservative routing — is a presentation invariant with a one-line runbook, not a security boundary with an exclusion doctrine.

**D6. On the objection channel.** The policy design builds an objection channel as its compiled form of modified g1, complete with sealed naming, halt integration, and its own failure modes (their F5) — all because its qualified path has no human brake. This direction's human brake is the gate itself: every deployment can be denied, in the existing ceremony, by a named human, recorded in the disposition store. The objection channel is a surface the prior design had to invent because it deleted the surface the gate already provided; the framing's guarantee (a) is exactly that surface, and this direction kept it, so it needs no replacement.

**D7. On security-capacity consumption — the sharpest comparative point.** The policy design records against itself (its D6) that it is the heaviest consumer of security-team time among the readings, from a pool the framing records as unsecured. The classify design consumes security for thresholds, override review, and the FNR loop. This direction consumes zero security capacity on its delivery path and near-zero in steady state. In an organization where reviewer capacity is fixed, security capacity is unsecured, and the framing explicitly says both constraints bind, a direction whose value is delivered by the staffing that already exists is not merely convenient — it is the only one of the three that can start this quarter on the org chart that exists this quarter.

**D8. Recorded against myself: the latencies and the A5 exposure.** Two places where the prior designs are honestly stronger, stated so the compaction does not have to discover them. First, the deployer-latency axis: both automation directions remove queue time for a large class; this direction plausibly adds a small mean wait for the routine class (the batch-cadence floor) and cannot remove the human queue at all. If the sponsor's retained objective is wait-time and the attention-quality reframe (decision 2) was cosmetic, this direction underdelivers and the prior designs are correct. I take decision 2 at its word and do not hedge the design to cover the rejection of it. Second, safety machinery: both prior directions carry a machine-checkable first-term safety boundary; this direction's entire safety increment rides on the framing's assumption A5 ("attention quality serves screening quality"), which the framing flags as unevidenced. The direction's mitigation is that A5 is testable in its own P0 data before any queue change ships — cheaper and sooner than either prior design's cutover evidence — but if A5 fails the test, the honest conclusion is that this direction's upside was always a bet on it, and the prior designs' automation bets would deserve their weight. I record the exposure rather than soften it.

**D9. On convergence.** D1–D7 are not points of tuning; they are a different diagnosis of where the problem lives — in the allocation of the decision, not in the decision itself. The prior designs are coherent, honest, and well-built on their premise; the premise is a bet on a license that has not been granted, paid for in a modified guarantee and a heavy operating surface. This direction makes the opposite bet with the framing's own endorsement behind it, and the record should show that they disagree at the root rather than only in the details.
